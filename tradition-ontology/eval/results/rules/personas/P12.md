## The Vedic and yogic reading

- Read through the Vedic and yogic texts, your words “What I actually want to look at is how I am with my team” come closest to what they call Āsurī sampad (the demonic endowment) (a partial match for Māna (conceit)). Hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness and ignorance belong to one born to the demonic endowment (BhG 16.4); it leads to bondage (16.5). _(Bhagavad Gītā 16.4; Bhagavad Gītā 16.5; Visuddhimagga 14.p469)_

## The ascetic reading (Buddhist and Jain)

- Your words “What I actually want to look at is how I am with my team” fit only loosely what the Buddhist texts call Māna (conceit). Vism XXII: the fetters are ten states, beginning with lust for form, so called because they bind aggregates to aggregates, action to its fruit, and beings to suffering. Lust for form, lust for the formless, conceit, restlessness and ignorance are the five higher fetters; personality view, doubt, adherence to rules and observances, sensual lust and resentment are the five lower fetters. _(Visuddhimagga 14.p469; Visuddhimagga 22.p682; Visuddhimagga 22.p683)_

## How the two readings fit together

- Each names a false or inflated 'I'. YS: I-am-ness is the seeming identity of the power of the seer and the power of seeing (YS 2.6). Vyāsa: the puruṣa is the seer's power, the buddhi the power of seeing (YB 2.6). Gītā: arrogance and conceit (darpa, abhimāna) belong to the demonic endowment (BhG 16.4), which leads to bondage (BhG 16.5). TS: pride (māna) is one of the four passions (TS 8.9), by which the soul takes in karmic matter (TS 8.2). Visuddhimagga: personality view (sakkāyadiṭṭhi) regards form and the other aggregates as self (Vism XVII) and is one of the five lower fetters; conceit (māna) is a defilement and a fetter that remains until the arahat path (Vism XXII). Under the one-truth principle, they describe it from different standpoints: the match is a partial match, not an identity. _(standpoint; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.6; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 2.6; Bhagavad Gītā 16.4; Bhagavad Gītā 16.5; Tattvārthasūtra 8.9; Tattvārthasūtra 8.2; Visuddhimagga 17.p570; Visuddhimagga 22.p683; Visuddhimagga 22.p682; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.5; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.25)_
- Where they differ: On the sense of 'I': Jain, Yoga, Upaniṣadic and Buddhist texts all teach that one is not the body, nor any passing state of mind. The Yoga Sūtra calls the seer's seeming oneness with the mind 'I-am-ness'; Theravāda texts teach seeing 'this is not mine, I am not this'. They differ on what is left: Jain texts, many souls whose changing states are their own; the Yoga Sūtra, many seers that never change; the Upaniṣads, a self that is brahman; Theravāda texts, nothing that is a self. These are different answers, not one. _(Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.10; Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.4.1-2; Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.8.7; Chāndogya Upaniṣad 8.12.1-2; Dhammapada 279; Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 2; Tattvārthasūtra 10.9; Tattvārthasūtra 2.1; Tattvārthasūtra 2.7; Tattvārthasūtra 7.12; Tattvārthasūtra 8.11; Tattvārthasūtra 8.2; Tattvārthasūtra 9.7; Visuddhimagga 20.p638; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 2.22; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 2.5; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 2.6; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.20; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.22; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.5; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 2.6; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 4.18)_
- Where they differ: Asmitā is not pride. YS 2.6 names a confusion of the seer with its instrument, a structural error that is present even without any haughtiness. The Gītā's darpa and abhimāna and the Jain māna are attitudes of self-exaltation: the Gītā lists them among the marks of a bad endowment (BhG 16.4), and TS grades māna in four intensities (TS 8.9). They meet only in the 'I'. Personality view comes closest to asmitā, since both take something that is not the self as the self. The Yoga also counts that as an error (YS 2.5), but it corrects the error by isolating a real seer (YS 2.25). The Visuddhimagga's remedy is to abandon the view, which is one of the five lower fetters, and conceit remains until the arahat path (Vism XXII). It does not isolate a seer in the view's place. ## A gentle practice pathway

### Observing the sense fields and their fetter (MN 10:40)

You wrote “What I actually want to look at is how I am with my team”. The texts pair what they call Māna (conceit) with this practice.

1. Sit comfortably.
2. Understand the eye, sights, and the fetter that arises dependent on both (MN 10:40).
3. Understand how a fetter not yet arisen comes to arise, how an arisen one is given up, and how once given up it does not arise again (MN 10:40).
4. Go through the ear and sounds, nose and smells, tongue and tastes, body and touches, mind and ideas in the same way (MN 10:40).
5. Close gently.

Length: 5–10 minutes, 1 time(s) a day (product default for a beginner; the text gives no length).

**The texts' caution:** The sutta's condition for every contemplation: one meditates 'keen, aware and mindful, rid of covetousness and displeasure for the world'. _(Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta mn10:3)_

**The texts' caution:** Nearest general caution in the same discourse family: 'There is no development of mindfulness of breathing for someone who is unmindful and lacks awareness' (said of breathing mindfulness). _(Ānāpānasati Sutta mn118:26)_

_Texts: Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta mn10:40; Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta mn10:41_
