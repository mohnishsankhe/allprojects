## The Vedic and yogic reading

- The Vedic and yogic texts have their own account of a pattern like the one above: Daurmanasya (dejection) (a partial match for Ārta-dhyāna (sorrowful dwelling)). Dejection is agitation of the mind from the thwarting of a wish (icchā-vighāta). Your words were matched to Ārta-dhyāna (sorrowful dwelling), not to this; it is shown so that both readings are in view. _(Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 1.31; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 1.31)_

## The ascetic reading (Buddhist and Jain)

- Your words “i want to go straight back to it” fit only loosely what the Jain texts call Ārta-dhyāna (sorrowful dwelling). Meditation (dhyāna) is of four kinds: sorrowful (ārta), cruel (raudra), virtuous (dharmya) and pure (śukla) (TS 9.28); the last two are causes of liberation (TS 9.29). _(Tattvārthasūtra 9.28; Tattvārthasūtra 9.29)_

## How the two readings fit together

- For what you wrote, “i want to go straight back to it”: Each names a sinking, sorrowful turn of mind. Vyāsa: dejection (daurmanasya) is a disturbance of the mind through the frustration of a wish, and it accompanies distraction (YS 1.31; YB 1.31). Gītā: Arjuna is overcome by pity and despairing (viṣīdan), his mind bewildered about dharma (BhG 1.28, 2.1, 2.7). TS: sorrowful dwelling (ārta-dhyāna) dwells on removing the disagreeable, on keeping the agreeable, on pain, and on longing for future enjoyment (TS 9.30–33). MN 10 declares the four establishments of mindfulness a path to the ending of pain and displeasure (dukkha-domanassa) (MN 10:2). Under the one-truth principle, they describe it from different standpoints: the match is a partial match, not an identity. _(standpoint; Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 1.31; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 1.31; Bhagavad Gītā 1.28; Bhagavad Gītā 2.1; Bhagavad Gītā 2.7; Tattvārthasūtra 9.30; Tattvārthasūtra 9.31; Tattvārthasūtra 9.32; Tattvārthasūtra 9.33; Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta mn10:2)_
- Where they differ: For what you wrote, “i want to go straight back to it”: (1) Daurmanasya in YS is a companion of distraction: it occurs in a distracted mind and is absent in a concentrated one (YB 1.31). (2) Arjuna's viṣāda is a crisis about dharma, and it becomes the occasion of the whole teaching (BhG 2.7). (3) Jain ārta-dhyāna is a kind of meditation (dhyāna), the inauspicious sorrowful one of four (TS 9.28): a sustained fixing of the mind, not a passing mood. This row describes states the texts name. It is not a finding about any person, and it makes no health claim. _(Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali 1.31; Yogabhāṣya (Vyāsa's commentary on the Yoga Sūtra) 1.31; Bhagavad Gītā 2.7; Tattvārthasūtra 9.28)_

## A gentle practice pathway

### The reflections (anuprekṣā, TS 9.7)

You wrote “i want to go straight back to it”. This reading suggests the practice below for what the texts call Ārta-dhyāna (sorrowful dwelling). The practice comes from the texts cited with it; the pairing with this pattern is this reading's, not theirs.

1. Sit comfortably.
2. Reflect repeatedly on impermanence (anitya), as the sūtra names it (TS 9.7).
3. Reflect on aloneness (ekatva) and on otherness (anyatva), as the sūtra names them (TS 9.7).
4. Reflect on the rarity of enlightenment (bodhi-durlabha) (TS 9.7).
5. Reflect on the well-taught nature of dharma (dharma-svākhyātatva) (TS 9.7).
6. Close gently.

Length: 5–10 minutes, 1 time(s) a day (product default for a beginner; the text gives no length).

**The texts' caution:** Nearest general caution in the Tattvārtha (the sūtra gives none for this practice): reflection must not turn into sorrowful brooding (ārta-dhyāna), which the sūtra defines as dwelling on removing the disagreeable, keeping the agreeable, on pain, and hankering for future enjoyment (nidāna). _(Tattvārthasūtra 9.30; Tattvārthasūtra 9.31; Tattvārthasūtra 9.32; Tattvārthasūtra 9.33)_
