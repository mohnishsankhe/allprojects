## 1. Opening and closing (upakrama / upasaṃhāra)
- **Frame opening (1.1):** Dhṛtarāṣṭra's question. The first word is *dharmakṣetre*, "on the field of dharma".
- **The problem the teaching answers** (A and B agree):
  - Arjuna's grief (śoka). He sits down "his mind overwhelmed by sorrow" (1.47), and no kingdom could dispel it (2.8).
  - His bewilderment about dharma (*dharma-saṃmūḍha-cetāḥ*, 2.7).
  - His request for what is decisively better (*śreyas*). The word first appears at 1.31; the request is at 2.7 and is repeated at 3.2 ("tell me decisively the one thing").
  - His refusal: *na yotsya*, "I will not fight" (2.9).
- **Opening of the teaching proper (2.11):** "you have grieved for those not to be grieved for … the wise do not grieve".
- **How each chapter in the chunk closes:**
  - Ch. 1 ends in śoka (1.47).
  - Ch. 2 ends with the *brāhmī sthiti*: no more delusion, and *brahmanirvāṇa* at death (2.72).
  - Ch. 3 ends: knowing what is beyond the understanding and steadying the self by the self, slay desire, the enemy (3.42–43).
  - 4.1 at once calls the teaching just given "this yoga", taught of old to Vivasvat (context only).
- **The text's closing mark lies outside this chunk.** For the thesis merger:
  - Compare 18.73 (*naṣṭo mohaḥ* …) with the opening *moha/śoka* of 1.47 and 2.7.
  - Arjuna's refusal (2.9) is not yet answered by his own assent within ch. 1–3.

## 3. Novelty (apūrvatā): A and B weigh this mark differently, and both views are kept
- **Agreed: the chunk's own marked step is 2.39,** "this was told you in sāṃkhya; now hear it in yoga". It introduces:
  - the understanding "yoked" in action, which casts off the bondage of action (2.39);
  - the right to the action, never to its fruit (2.47);
  - yoga as sameness (2.48) and as skill in actions (2.50).

  It is set explicitly against those who delight in the words of the Veda, say "there is nothing else", and whose Vedas have the three guṇas as their domain (2.42–46).
- **Agreed: the text claims antiquity, not newness, for the twofold commitment** (*purā proktā mayā*, 3.3; cf. 4.1–3). The explicit novelty and secrecy marks for this yoga fall in the next chunk: 4.1–3, Vivasvat, *yogo naṣṭaḥ*, *rahasyaṃ hy etad uttamam*.
- **A counts as novel:**
  - That the self is not known by ordinary means. It is seen, spoken of and heard of "as a wonder", yet "even having heard, no one knows it" (2.29). It is unmanifest and unthinkable (2.25) and immeasurable (*aprameya*, 2.18).
  - As against the ritual view:
    - action done without claim on its fruit does not bind (2.47–51);
    - actionlessness is not reached by not acting (3.4);
    - the Vedas "have the three guṇas as their domain" (2.45).
- **B holds that the teaching on the self is not new.** It is given in Upaniṣadic wording:
  - 2.19–20 ≈ Kaṭha 1.2.19 and 1.2.18;
  - 2.29 ≈ Kaṭha 1.2.7;
  - 3.42 ≈ Kaṭha 1.3.10;
  - *ātmarati* (3.17), as in ChU 7.25.2 and MuU 3.1.4.

  What B finds new relative to these parallels:
  - Kṛṣṇa as the one the practitioner is oriented to (*matparaḥ*, 2.61; *mayi sarvāṇi karmāṇi saṃnyasya*, 3.30);
  - his own example of acting with nothing to gain (3.22–24).
- **Merger:** the two views do not exclude each other. A measures novelty against ordinary knowledge and against the ritual view; B measures it against the Upaniṣads. For the Mīmāṃsā sense of apūrvatā (not known from another source), B's parallels matter. The thesis merger should weigh both.

## 4. Stated result (phala)
- **Liberation and inner results:**
  - fitness for immortality (*amṛtatva*, 2.15);
  - casting off the bondage of action (*karma-bandha*, 2.39);
  - no effort lost, no adverse result, and protection from great fear (2.40);
  - casting off good and evil deeds (2.50);
  - freedom from the bondage of birth and reaching "the state free from ill" (*padam anāmayam*, 2.51);
  - attaining yoga (2.53);
  - serenity (*prasāda*), in which all sorrows end and understanding steadies (2.64–65);
  - peace (*śānti*, 2.66, 2.70–71);
  - the *brāhmī sthiti*: no more delusion, and *brahmanirvāṇa* at death (2.72);
  - freedom from all faults for those who eat the remainder of the sacrifice (3.13);
  - reaching the highest (*param*, 3.19);
  - perfection (*saṃsiddhi*), as with Janaka (3.20);
  - freedom from actions for those who follow the teaching with faith and without cavilling (3.31).
- **Ritual and conventional results:**
  - the highest good through gods and humans nourishing each other (3.11);
  - within the svadharma argument: the open door to heaven (2.32), heaven if slain and the earth if victorious (2.37), and no sin (2.38).
- **Negative results:**
  - sin and disgrace for abandoning one's own dharma (2.33–36);
  - the non-sacrificer "lives in vain" (3.16);
  - those who cavil are "lost" (3.32).

## 5. Praise and blame (arthavāda)
- **Praise:**
  - happy the kṣatriyas who meet such a battle, "an open door to heaven" (2.32);
  - "even a little of this dharma saves from great fear" (2.40);
  - the sage awake in the night of beings (2.69) [B];
  - the ocean, filled yet unmoved (2.70).
- **Blame:**
  - disgrace worse than death, and the scorn of enemies (2.34–36);
  - the "flowery speech" of the unwise who delight in Vedic words and are "desire-souled" (2.42–44);
  - "pitiable are those whose motive is the fruit" (2.49);
  - the hypocrite (3.6);
  - one who enjoys the gods' gifts without offering to them is "a thief" (3.12);
  - those who cook for themselves "eat sin" (3.13);
  - one who does not turn the wheel of sacrifice "lives in vain" (3.16);
  - those who carp at the teaching are "lost" (3.32).
- **The well in a flood (2.46)** is read in opposite directions by the commentators: as blame of the Veda's limits, or as praise of the knower. A lists it under blame; both note the division.
- **Arjuna's appeals to hell and to the fall of the ancestors** rest on received tradition ("so we have heard", 1.42–44).
  - A: they work as blame within his own argument.
  - B: they belong to the view Kṛṣṇa is answering, not to Kṛṣṇa's teaching.
- **Candidates for the arthavāda principle (P7) in the interpretation layer** [B]:
  - the appeals to heaven and honour in 2.32–37, which 2.38 and 2.47 at once reframe (the fruit is not to be the motive);
  - Arjuna's appeals of 1.42–44.

## 6. Reasoned demonstration (upapatti)
- **From the nature of the embodied one (2.12–25),** so grief is misplaced:
  - the eternal existence of "I, you and these kings" (2.12);
  - the analogy of the ages of life (2.13);
  - *sat* and *asat* (2.16);
  - that by which all is pervaded (2.17);
  - it neither slays nor is slain (2.19–21);
  - the garments simile (2.22);
  - the elements cannot affect it (2.23–24).
- **On the opposite assumption (2.26–28):** even if the self is born and dies again and again, death is certain for the born and beings are unmanifest at beginning and end, so grief is still unfit.
- **From one's own dharma and honour** (2.31–37).
- **Causal chains in the mind:**
  - from dwelling on objects down to ruin (2.62–63);
  - from mastered senses up to serenity and peace (2.64–66);
  - the ship-and-wind image (2.67).
- **Why one must act:**
  - no one can remain without action, since the guṇas make all act (3.5);
  - even bodily life needs action (3.8);
  - the wheel of sacrifice: beings, food, rain, sacrifice, action, brahman, the imperishable (3.14–16);
  - the argument from example: Janaka (3.20), the best person as the standard (3.21), and Kṛṣṇa's own action (3.22–24).
- **Analysis:**
  - the guṇas are the doers, and "I am the doer" is the delusion of ahaṃkāra (3.27–29);
  - the three images of covering: smoke, dirt on a mirror, the womb-membrane (3.38);
  - the hierarchy senses < mind < understanding < "he" (3.42).

## What the chunk suggests for the text-level thesis (for the last-chunk merger)
- **Movement (A and B agree):** from Arjuna's grief and his confusion about dharma to two answers.
  - Knowledge of the imperishable embodied one ("in sāṃkhya", 2.11–30).
  - Action without attachment to its fruit ("in yoga", from 2.39). It is done as one's own dharma, as sacrifice and for holding the world together, and is finally resigned to Kṛṣṇa (3.30).

  Desire is the enemy to be overcome (3.36–43). The goals named are steady wisdom, peace, freedom from the bondage of action and of birth, reaching the highest, and brahmanirvāṇa.
- **Turning points where the chunk restates its point** (A): 2.18, 2.38, 2.47–48, 3.8, 3.19, 3.30.
- **Open question (A and B agree):** Arjuna himself states the tension between knowledge and action (3.1–2). Kṛṣṇa answers with:
  - the twofold commitment (3.3–4);
  - the impossibility of not acting (3.5);
  - the knower "for whom nothing remains to be done" (3.17–18), who is nevertheless told to act (3.19).

  How these fit together is where the commentators divide (dsp:renunciation-or-action-gita, dsp:works-knowledge-grace, dsp:gita-primary-teaching). Ch. 1–3 alone should not settle it.
- **Debates within the text recorded in this chunk** (merged ids):
  - dsp:bhagavad-gita-arjuna-should-fight: Arjuna 1.28–2.9 against Kṛṣṇa 2.2–3.35; the broader epic debate is dsp:violence-and-svadharma.
  - dsp:bhagavad-gita-vedavada: 2.42–46; the vedavādins are known only through the Gītā's report.
  - dsp:renunciation-or-action-gita: 3.1–26; this reuses the skeleton's id.
