# Bhagavad Gītā ch. 1–3 — extractor A: what this chunk contributes to the six marks of purport (ṣaḍ-liṅga)
_(Returned as text by the subagent — subagents cannot write Markdown files in this harness — and saved verbatim by the orchestrator.)_


## 1. Opening and closing (upakrama / upasaṃhāra)
- **Opening of the text:** the first word is *dharmakṣetre* (1.1). The crisis is Arjuna's grief (śoka 1.47, 2.8) and his confusion about dharma (*dharma-saṃmūḍha-cetāḥ*, 2.7). He asks what is better (*śreyas*): 1.31, 2.7 and again 3.2 ("tell me decisively the one thing").
- **Opening of the teaching proper:** 2.11, "the wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the living".
- **Chunk-internal closings:**
  - Ch. 2 ends with the brahmic state and brahma-nirvāṇa at death (2.72).
  - Ch. 3 ends with the call to slay desire, the enemy, by knowing what is beyond understanding (3.43).
  - 4.1 at once calls the teaching just given "this yoga", told of old to Vivasvat (context only).
- The text's final closing (ch. 18) is outside this chunk. Arjuna's refusal ("I will not fight", 2.9) is not yet answered by his own assent within ch. 1–3.

## 2. Repetition (abhyāsa)
- "You ought not to grieve" (*na śocitum arhasi*): 2.25, 2.26, 2.27, 2.30 (also 2.11).
- "His wisdom is firmly established" (*tasya prajñā pratiṣṭhitā*): 2.57, 2.58, 2.61, 2.68.
- The command to act or fight (*yudhyasva / uttiṣṭha / kuru karma*): 2.3, 2.18, 2.37, 2.38, 2.48, 3.8, 3.9, 3.19, 3.30.
- Non-attachment (*saṅgaṃ tyaktvā / asakta / mukta-saṅga*) and giving up the fruit: 2.47, 2.48, 2.51, 3.7, 3.9, 3.19, 3.25.
- Understanding / buddhi-yoga: 2.39, 2.41, 2.44, 2.49, 2.50, 2.51, 2.52, 2.53; taken up again in Arjuna's question 3.1.
- Sameness (*samatva*): 2.15, 2.38, 2.48 (cf. 2.57).
- The imperishable embodied one (*dehin*): 2.13, 2.18, 2.20–25, 2.30.
- One's own dharma (*svadharma*): 2.31, 2.33, 3.35.
- Desire (*kāma*): 2.55, 2.62, 2.70–71, 3.37–43.
- Restraint of the senses: 2.58, 2.61, 2.64, 2.68, 3.7, 3.41.

## 3. Novelty (apūrvatā)
- **The self is not known by ordinary means:** it is seen, spoken of and heard of "as a wonder", yet "even having heard, no one knows it" (2.29). It is also called unmanifest and unthinkable (2.25) and immeasurable (*aprameya*, 2.18).
- **A new way of teaching is announced:** "this was told you in sāṃkhya; now hear it in yoga" (2.39). The new element is understanding "yoked" in action, which casts off the bondage of action.
- **Novel as against the ritual view:**
  - Action does not bind when done without claim on its fruit (2.47–51).
  - Actionlessness is not reached by not acting (3.4).
  - The Vedas "have the three guṇas as their domain" (2.45).
- **What the text claims for itself is antiquity, not newness:** the twofold commitment was "declared by me of old" (3.3; cf. 4.1–3).

## 4. Stated result (phala)
- **For the teaching:**
  - fit for immortality (2.15);
  - casting off the bondage of action (2.39);
  - no loss of effort, no adverse result, protection from great fear (2.40);
  - leaving behind good and evil deeds (2.50);
  - freedom from the bondage of birth and reaching the state free from ill (*padam anāmayam*) (2.51);
  - attaining yoga (2.53);
  - serenity and the end of all sorrows (2.64–65);
  - peace (2.70–71);
  - brahma-nirvāṇa (2.72);
  - release from all faults (3.13);
  - the highest (3.19);
  - perfection, as with Janaka (3.20);
  - release from actions for those who follow the teaching with faith (3.31).
- **Conventional results:** heaven or the earth for the kṣatriya (2.32, 2.37); the highest good through gods and humans nourishing each other (3.11).
- **Negative results:** sin and disgrace for abandoning one's own dharma (2.33–36); the cavillers "lost" (3.32); the non-sacrificer "lives in vain" (3.16).

## 5. Praise and blame (arthavāda)
- **Praise:**
  - the dharmic battle as an "open door to heaven" (2.32);
  - "even a little of this dharma saves from great fear" (2.40);
  - the ocean simile (2.70).
- **Blame:**
  - disgrace worse than death (2.34–36);
  - the "flowery speech" of the ritual-minded, who are "desire-souled" (2.42–44);
  - the well amid a flood (2.46), which is read in opposite directions;
  - "wretched are those whose motive is the fruit" (2.49);
  - the one who enjoys without offering is "a thief" (3.12), and those who cook only for themselves "eat sin" (3.13);
  - the hypocrite (3.6).
- **On Arjuna's side:** his appeals to hell and to the fall of the ancestors rest on received tradition ("so we have heard", 1.42–44). They work as blame within his own argument.

## 6. Reasoned demonstration (upapatti)
- **From the nature of the self (2.12–25):** the self is eternal, passes through bodies, and neither slays nor is slain, so grief is misplaced.
- **From the opposite view (2.26–28):** even if the self is born and dies, death is certain for the born, and beings are unmanifest at their beginning and end.
- **From one's own dharma and honour (2.31–37).**
- **Why one must act:**
  - no one can stay without action, because the guṇas act (3.5);
  - the body itself needs action (3.8);
  - the wheel of sacrifice (food ← rain ← sacrifice ← action ← brahman ← the imperishable, 3.14–16);
  - people follow the example of the best (3.21), and Kṛṣṇa himself acts though he needs nothing (3.22–24);
  - the guṇas are the doers and "I am the doer" is delusion (3.27–29).
- **Two causal chains:** from dwelling on objects down to ruin (2.62–63), and from mastered senses up to serenity (2.64–65).
- **A hierarchy:** senses < mind < understanding < "he" (3.42).

## What the chunk suggests for the text-level thesis (for the last-chunk merger)
- The chunk keeps returning to one point, stated at its turning points (2.18, 2.38, 2.47–48, 3.8, 3.19, 3.30). Grounded in knowledge of the imperishable embodied self, one does one's own dharma. One acts with steady, resolute understanding (buddhi-yoga), without attachment to the fruit, and in the end resigns action to Kṛṣṇa. One overcomes desire. The results named are steady wisdom, peace and brahma-nirvāṇa.
- The chunk itself leaves one question open for the thesis. Arjuna asks at 3.1–2 how knowledge relates to action, and Kṛṣṇa answers with the twofold commitment (3.3) and the knower "for whom nothing remains to be done" (3.17–18). How those fit together is a point on which the commentators divide (see dsp:works-knowledge-grace), and the thesis should not settle it from ch. 1–3 alone.
