The most influential Buddhist epistemologist, author of 'seven treatises on valid cognition': he grounded inference in necessary connection by identity or causality, recast exclusion, proved momentariness from causal efficacy, argued for rebirth and for the Buddha as a person of valid cognition, and refuted God and the authorless Veda; one of the Tibetan 'six ornaments'.
**Realization — the tradition's account (Bu ston; Tāranātha):** Born to a brahmin family in the south, he mastered the Vedas and, disillusioned, took up Buddhism; to learn his opponents' doctrine he served in disguise in the household of the Mīmāṃsaka Kumārila; he heard the Pramāṇasamuccaya from Īśvarasena, understood it better than his teacher, and with his permission composed the Pramāṇavārttika, defeating many opponents in debate; he died in Kaliṅga.

_Notes: The Kumārila service story is chronologically doubtful and is the tradition's._
