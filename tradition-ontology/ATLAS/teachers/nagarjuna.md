Founder of the Madhyamaka: author of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and the other 'reasoning' treatises, of letters to a Sātavāhana king and of hymns, revered in every Mahāyāna tradition as the 'second Buddha' who opened the Perfection of Wisdom teaching of emptiness.
**Realization — the tradition's account:** The Laṅkāvatāra prophesies that a monk named Nāga will destroy the views of existence and non-existence, teach the supreme Mahāyāna and reach the Joyful ground (pramuditā bhūmi) before going to Sukhāvatī. The Chinese Life tells that as a brahmin youth he misused an art of invisibility, narrowly escaped death, renounced, exhausted the available scriptures and received the Mahāyāna sūtras from a great nāga in the nāga palace. Tibetan histories (Bu-ston, Tāranātha) tell that he was ordained at Nālandā, became its abbot, brought the Perfection of Wisdom in 100,000 lines back from the nāgas, practised alchemy, advised a Sātavāhana king and at the end gave his head to a prince who severed it with a blade of kuśa grass.

_Notes: Scholarly account: a South Indian monk of c. 150–250 CE connected with the Sātavāhana court; the name was borne also by later authors (a tantric Nāgārjuna and the alchemist tch:nagarjuna-siddha), which the tradition conflates. The 'reasoning corpus' (MMK, VV, ŚS, YṢ, VP) and the RĀ and SL are widely accepted; other attributions are disputed. Laṅkāvatāra prophecy verse number (c. 10.165–166) to check._
