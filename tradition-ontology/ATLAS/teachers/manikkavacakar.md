# Māṇikkavācakar

`tch:manikkavacakar` · `skeleton` · confidence high

**Alternate names:** Tiruvātavūrar (Vātavūrar), Māṇikkavācaka ('he whose words are rubies')
**Lineages:** [The Nāyaṉmārs and the Tirumuṟai (Tamil Śaiva bhakti)](../lineages/nayanmar.md), [Tamil Śaiva Siddhānta (the Meykaṇṭa school)](../lineages/tamil-saiva-siddhanta.md)
**Dates:** Tradition's account: placed before the Tēvāram poets by much of the tradition; Scholarly account: c. 9th c.; (confidence moderate)
**Places:** Tiruvātavūr (birth), Madurai, Tirupperunturai (Āvuṭaiyārkōyil), Chidambaram
**Historicity:** historical
**Works:** 
  - [Tiruvācakam](../texts/tiruvacakam.md) — attribution: accepted
  - [Tirukkōvaiyār](../texts/tirukkovaiyar.md) — attribution: accepted

Author of the Tiruvācakam and Tirukkōvaiyār (Tirumuṟai 8); the fourth samayācārya, exemplar of the true path (san-mārga, jñāna). Not among the sixty-three.
**Realization — the tradition's account:** Chief minister of the Pāṇṭiya king (named Arimarttaṉa in later accounts), sent with treasure to buy horses, he met Śiva as a guru under a kuruntha tree at Tirupperunturai, received initiation and spent the treasure on a temple. Imprisoned, he was saved when Śiva turned foxes into horses (which became foxes again), and when Śiva, hired as a labourer by the old woman Vanti to repair the Vaikai embankment, received the king's blow that struck every creature. At Chidambaram he defeated Buddhists from Laṅkā in debate; Śiva, as a brahmin, wrote down the Tiruvācakam and signed it; asked its meaning, he pointed to Naṭarāja and merged in him.

_Notes: His absence from Cuntarar's list is used by scholars to date him after Cuntarar._
