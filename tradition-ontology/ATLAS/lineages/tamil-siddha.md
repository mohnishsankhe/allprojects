**Status:** living

A loose, non-institutional stream of Tamil perfected ones (cittar) — yogins, poets, physicians and alchemists — counted conventionally as eighteen, with lists that vary. They trace their knowledge to Śiva through Nandi, Agastya and Tirumūlar, teach an inner Śaiva-Śākta yoga of breath, the root centre and the goddess Vālai aimed at a perfected, deathless body and union with Civam, and in their songs attack caste, idols, temples, pilgrimage and Brahmanical ritual. Their songs (Sivavākkiyar, Pāmbāṭṭi, Iṭaikkāṭar, Kuṭampai, Aḻukaṇṇi, Kaṭuveḷi, Pattinattār, Pattirakiriyār) are anthologized as the Siddhar songs (Cittar pāṭalkaḷ); Tāyumāṉavar (18th c.) joins Siddha, Vedānta and Siddhānta. Siddha medicine is their medical branch.

## Distinctive positions
- The body is the temple and the instrument of liberation: it is to be preserved and perfected (kāya-siddhi, kāya kaṟpam), not despised — 'when the body perishes the life perishes and true knowledge is not reached' (TM 724).
- God (Civam) is within; love itself is Śiva (TM 270); the stone image, temple, pilgrimage, Vedic recitation and ritual without inner knowledge are mocked (Sivavākkiyar; Kaṭuveḷi 15; Pattirakiriyār 16; Kuṭampai).
- Caste is rejected: 'one family, one God' (TM 2104); the castes said to be unequal are one (Kaṭuveḷi 32).
- Breath (vāci), the root centre (mūlam), the six supports (ātāram), the goddess Vālai/Maṉōṉmaṇi and the open space above the crown (veṭṭa veḷi) frame an inner yoga that culminates in 'dying without dying' (cākāmal cātal).
- Teaching in veiled, coded language (paripāṣai, maṟaipporuḷ), with secrecy around alchemy and medicine.
- Mastery of the powers (citti) is claimed, yet stillness of mind is held harder and higher (Tāyumāṉavar; Kuṭampai 2).
- The 96 principles (toṇṇūṟṟāṟu tattuvam) map body, mind and bondage; medicine and liberation share one anatomy.
