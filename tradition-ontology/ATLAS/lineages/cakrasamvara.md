The Yoginī-tantra tradition centred on Heruka Cakrasaṃvara and Vajravārāhī, based on the Laghuśaṃvara and its explanatory tantras, with the twenty-four sacred sites (shared with Śaiva pīṭha lists) mapped onto the body, and three Indian practice lineages (Lūipa, Ghaṇṭāpa, Kāṇha) that passed to Tibet and the Newars.

## Distinctive positions
- Heruka (Cakrasaṃvara) in union with Vajravārāhī is the embodiment of great bliss and emptiness.
- The maṇḍala's twenty-four sacred sites (pīṭha etc.) are located in the outer land and, inwardly, in the yogin's body as channels (the body maṇḍala).
- Three Indian lineages of practice: Lūipa's (with Vajravārāhī's cycle), Ghaṇṭāpa's (the five-deity body maṇḍala) and Kāṇha's (Kṛṣṇācārya's).
- The Vajrayoginī (Vajravārāhī) sādhanas of Nāropa, Indrabhūti and Maitrīpa belong to this cycle.

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Śrī Heruka / Cakrasaṃvara with Vajravārāhī, mahāsukha (great bliss), saṃvara ('supreme bliss' / binding)
**Descriptions:** the union of Heruka (means, bliss) and Vajravārāhī (wisdom, emptiness); the supreme bliss (śrīcakrasaṃvara) that is the 'binding' of the wheel of all phenomena
**Relation to self:** The yogin's body is the maṇḍala: the twenty-four sites are its channels and the deities reside in it (body maṇḍala).
**Relation to world:** The land of India (the pīṭhas) is the outer maṇḍala; worlds and bodies correspond.
**Caveat:** The etymology 'saṃvara = supreme bliss' is the commentators'; the tradition keeps Heruka empty of own-being and distinguishes him from Śaiva Bhairava, whom he is said to have subdued.
