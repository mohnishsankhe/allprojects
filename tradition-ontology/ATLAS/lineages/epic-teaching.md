**Status:** absorbed

The teaching layer of the two Sanskrit epics, above all the Mahābhārata: the Bhagavad Gītā, Bhīṣma's instruction in the Śāntiparvan (Rājadharma, Āpaddharma, Mokṣadharma) and Anuśāsanaparvan, the Anugītā, the Sanatsujātīya, Vidura's counsel, the Yakṣa's questions, Mārkaṇḍeya's discourses, and the dharma teaching of the Rāmāyaṇa. It is not a school with a single doctrine but a milieu in which Upaniṣadic, early Sāṃkhya and Yoga, Vedic ritual, devotional (Bhāgavata) and ascetic ideas are set side by side in dialogues, and which later schools (Vedānta, classical Sāṃkhya and Yoga, the Purāṇas, bhakti traditions) all claim as authority.

## Distinctive positions
- Action is unavoidable for the embodied; what binds is attachment to the fruit, not action itself (BhG 3.5, 2.47, 18.11).
- Several disciplines — knowledge (sāṃkhya), disciplined action (yoga), meditation and devotion — lead to one goal; 'one who sees Sāṃkhya and Yoga as one, sees' (BhG 3.3, 5.4–5, 13.24–25).
- The self in the body is unborn, eternal, not slain when the body is slain (BhG 2.12–30).
- Prakṛti with its three guṇas is the agent of all action; the puruṣa experiences and witnesses (BhG 3.27, 13.19–22).
- The principles are counted as 24 with the puruṣa as 25th, and the Vasiṣṭha–Karāla and Yājñavalkya dialogues add a 26th, the awakened (MBh 12.294–296, 12.306).
- Sāṃkhya and Yoga differ in method — Yoga relies on direct perception and argues that without a Lord no one is freed; Sāṃkhya relies on the teaching of the śāstra — yet both are true (MBh 12.289.3–7).
- Dharma is subtle: conduct, not birth alone, is said to make a brāhmaṇa (3.177.16, 12.181.10), while the duties of the varṇas are also upheld (BhG 18.41–48; 12.60).
- Non-cruelty (ānṛśaṃsya) and non-violence (ahiṃsā) are called the highest dharma (3.297.55; 1.11.12; 13.116), alongside the kṣatriya's duty to fight a righteous war (BhG 2.31–33).
- Liberation (brahmanirvāṇa, entering the Lord) can be won in this life by one whose mind rests in equality (BhG 5.19, 5.26, 2.72).
- Time (kāla), nature (svabhāva), fate (daiva) and human effort are debated as causes; the Gītā counts daiva as one of five causes of action (18.14) and Bhīṣma teaches that fate does not succeed without effort (13.6.7).
- Dharma, artha and kāma are one whole (trivarga) grounded in dharma; 'from dharma come artha and kāma — why is it not practised?' (18.5.49).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Brahman — beginningless, supreme, imperishable (anādimat paraṃ brahma; akṣara), Puruṣottama, the highest person beyond the perishable and the imperishable, Paramātman, the supreme self in the body, Īśvara / Maheśvara, the Lord dwelling in the heart of all beings, Vāsudeva — 'Vāsudeva is all' (BhG 7.19), the unmanifest (avyakta) beyond the unmanifest, the twenty-sixth, the awakened (buddha), beyond the twenty-five, Time (kāla), the destroyer of worlds (BhG 11.32)
**Descriptions:** beginningless supreme Brahman, said to be neither being nor non-being (BhG 13.12); with hands and feet everywhere, eyes, heads and faces everywhere, it stands enveloping all (13.13); the light of lights beyond darkness, knowledge, the known and the goal of knowledge, seated in the heart of all (13.17); that by which all this is pervaded is indestructible (2.17); the highest Person who, entering the three worlds, sustains them (15.17–18); the origin and dissolution of the whole world; all this is strung on him like pearls on a thread (7.6–7); the knower of the field in all fields (13.2); the twenty-sixth, ever awake, which the twenty-fifth comes to know (MBh 12.294–296)
**Negations:** neither being nor non-being — na sat tan nāsad ucyate (BhG 13.12); unmanifest, unthinkable, unchangeable (2.25); weapons do not cut it, fire does not burn it (2.23–24); not seen with the ordinary eye, nor by Vedas, sacrifice, study or austerity, but by a divine eye or undivided devotion (11.8, 11.48, 11.53–54); all beings abide in me but I do not abide in them — and yet beings do not abide in me (9.4–5); the sun, moon and fire do not illumine that supreme abode (15.6)
**Relation to self:** The self in every body is unborn and eternal (BhG 2.20); the Lord is the knower of the field in all fields (13.2) and an eternal portion of him becomes the living being (15.7); the supreme person in the body is witness, permitter, supporter and experiencer (13.22). The Mokṣadharma distinguishes the twenty-fifth (the self coming to awakening) from the twenty-sixth (the awakened) and teaches that the knower attains oneness (12.294–296). The commentators read these relations as identity (Advaita), the Lord as inner self of the selves (Viśiṣṭādvaita) or eternal difference (Dvaita).
**Relation to world:** The Lord's lower, eightfold nature (earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect, ego) and his higher nature, the life-element, are the womb of all beings (BhG 7.4–6); at the end of each kalpa beings enter his prakṛti and at its beginning he sends them forth, prakṛti producing the moving and unmoving under his oversight (9.7–10); the world of saṃsāra is an aśvattha tree rooted above (15.1–3); he descends age after age to protect dharma (4.7–8).
**Caveat:** The epic milieu is plural: it records Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Vedic, Pāñcarātra and Pāśupata views (MBh 12.337.59), differing counts of principles, and the question whether puruṣas are many or one (12.338). BhG 12.1–5 holds that worshippers of the unmanifest imperishable and of the Lord in form both reach him, the former with greater difficulty. Each Vedānta school reads the Gītā's ultimate in its own way and rejects the others' readings; the one-truth reading belongs to the interpretation layer, not to the epic's own text.
