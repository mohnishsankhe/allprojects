Nyāya ('method', 'reasoning') is the Vedic darśana of the means of valid knowledge and of reasoned debate, founded on Gautama Akṣapāda's Nyāya Sūtra and developed by Vātsyāyana, Uddyotakara, Vācaspati Miśra, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa, Bhāsarvajña and Udayana. It teaches that liberation — absolute release from pain — follows from knowledge of the truth about the self and the other objects of knowledge, won by the means of knowledge, reasoning, study, discussion and yoga. From about the 10th–11th c. it merged with Vaiśeṣika into a single realist system, and from Gaṅgeśa (14th c.) continued as Navya-Nyāya.

## Distinctive positions
- The highest good (niḥśreyasa) is attained from knowledge of the truth of sixteen categories (NS 1.1.1), above all of the twelve objects of knowledge beginning with the self (NS 1.1.9).
- Bondage runs from false knowledge through faults, activity and birth to pain; removing false knowledge by knowledge of truth removes the rest and yields liberation (NS 1.1.2).
- Four means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison, verbal testimony (NS 1.1.3); tradition, presumption, inclusion and absence are reduced to these (NS 2.2.1–2).
- The self is an eternal, all-pervading substance distinct from body, senses, mind and cognition; desire, aversion, effort, pleasure, pain and cognition are its marks (NS 1.1.10); selves are many.
- The mind (manas) is one per body and atomic, which is why cognitions do not arise simultaneously (NS 1.1.16, 3.2.56, 3.2.59).
- Liberation (apavarga) is absolute release from pain (NS 1.1.22), in which — on the mainstream view — the self has neither pleasure nor cognition (NBh 1.1.22); Bhāsarvajña dissents, holding that eternal bliss is experienced.
- Validity of cognition is extrinsic (parataḥ); error is misplaced apprehension (anyathākhyāti).
- Īśvara is a distinct self, free of demerit, false knowledge and heedlessness, who assists human effort and makes action fruitful (NS 4.1.19–21; NBh); later he is proved by inference as maker of the world (Udayana, NK 5.1) and as author of the Veda, whose validity is like that of mantra and medicine because its speaker is reliable (NS 2.1.69).
- Effects are new beginnings not pre-existing in their causes (ārambha / asatkārya); the whole is a new substance distinct from its parts (NS 2.1.31–37, 4.2.4–17).
- Realism: external objects, universals, relations and enduring things are real; momentariness, idealism and the thesis that everything lacks own-nature are refuted (NS 3.2.10–17, 4.1.25–40, 4.2.26–37).
- A developed theory of debate: honest discussion (vāda), wrangling (jalpa) and cavilling (vitaṇḍā), with pseudo-reasons, quibbles, futile rejoinders and points of defeat; jalpa and vitaṇḍā are permitted to protect truth like a thorn-fence around a sprout (NS 4.2.50).
- Sound is a non-eternal quality of ākāśa; the relation of word and meaning is conventional (NS 2.1.56–57, 2.2.13ff.).
