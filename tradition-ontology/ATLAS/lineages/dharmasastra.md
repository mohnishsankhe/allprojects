The literature and learned tradition that set out dharma — ritual, moral, social and legal duty — for the twice-born and for all: the Dharmasūtras of Āpastamba, Gautama, Baudhāyana and Vasiṣṭha; the metrical smṛtis of Manu, Yājñavalkya, Nārada, Viṣṇu, Parāśara, Bṛhaspati and Kātyāyana; and the commentaries and digests (nibandha) built on them. Its core teachings are the four classes and four life-stages, the four aims of life, the three debts, the five great daily sacrifices, the rites of passage from conception to cremation, the ancestral offerings, purity and impurity, expiation, kingship and law, and — at its summit — self-knowledge and liberation.

## Distinctive positions
- The sources of dharma are the whole Veda, the recollection (smṛti) and conduct of those who know it, the practice of good people (sadācāra) and what satisfies oneself (MDh 2.6, 2.12; YS 1.7 adds desire born of right intention); where the Veda is silent, the agreement of those who know dharma is authoritative (ĀpDh 1.1.1.2).
- Dharma is specified by class (varṇa) and life-stage (āśrama); alongside these stands a dharma common to all four classes — non-injury, truth, not stealing, purity and restraint of the senses (MDh 10.63; YS 1.122).
- A person is born owing three debts — to the seers, the gods and the ancestors (TS 6.3.10.5; four in ŚB 1.7.2.1) — and must discharge them before setting the mind on liberation (MDh 6.35–37).
- The householder supports the other orders as air supports living beings (MDh 3.77–78; 6.89–90); whether the celibate orders are a free choice, a sequence, or not Vedic at all is disputed within the tradition (GDh 3.1, 3.35; BDh 2.6.11.27; VDh 7.3; MDh 6).
- Life is consecrated by rites (saṃskāra) from conception to cremation, and sins are removed by expiation, confession, remorse, austerity and recitation (MDh 2.26–28; 11.227–229).
- The highest dharma is seeing the self by yoga (YS 1.8); knowledge of the self is the highest of all practices, by which immortality is won (MDh 12.85).
- Acts of mind, speech and body bear fruit in rebirth, and the three guṇas govern the resulting destinies (MDh 12.3–51).
- Dharma differs from age to age (MDh 1.81–86; Parāśarasmṛti 1.24); later authorities list practices forbidden in the Kali age (kalivarjya).
- The customs of regions, castes and families are authoritative where they do not conflict with the Veda (GDh 11.20; ĀśGS 1.7.1); whether regional customs that the śiṣṭas disapprove of are valid is debated (BDh 1.1.2.1–8).
