The Jain tradition of the Jinas ('conquerors'), re-proclaimed in this era by Ṛṣabha and, last, by Vardhamāna Mahāvīra, a contemporary of the Buddha. It teaches the eternal reality of innumerable souls and of matter, karma as subtle matter binding the soul through passion and activity, and liberation through the three jewels, austerity and non-harm. It divided into the Śvetāmbara ('white-clad') and Digambara ('sky-clad') traditions, with the Yāpanīya between them, and within the Śvetāmbaras into image-worshipping and non-image-worshipping orders.

## Distinctive positions
- Innumerable individual souls (jīva), each by nature endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and energy, obscured by karma conceived as subtle matter that binds to the soul
- No creator or controlling God; the universe (loka) is uncreated and eternal, running through endless ascending and descending time-cycles
- Non-harm (ahiṃsā) toward all six classes of living beings, including earth-, water-, fire-, wind- and plant-bodied beings, as the pure and eternal law proclaimed by all Arhats
- Liberation by right faith, right knowledge and right conduct (with austerity), by one's own effort; the liberated soul (siddha) rises to the summit of the universe and remains individual
- Twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras ('ford-makers') in each half-cycle, who found the fourfold community (monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen)
- Many-sidedness (anekānta), standpoints (naya) and conditional predication (syādvāda) as the way of stating truth
- Voluntary, peaceful death by gradual fasting (sallekhanā) as the culmination of a disciplined life — under strict conditions (restricted; summary only)
