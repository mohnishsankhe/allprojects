The Pāśupatas are the earliest Śaiva ascetic order known from texts. They trace their teaching to Lakulīśa, Śiva's incarnation at Kāyāvarohaṇa in Gujarat, and to his four disciples Kuśika, Garga, Mitra and Kauruṣya. Their root text, the Pāśupata Sūtra, survives with Kauṇḍinya's Pañcārthabhāṣya, which organises it by five categories and five stages of practice. Initiated brahmin ascetics bathe and lie in ash, worship with laughter, song, dance, a bull-like sound, prostration and mantra, then leave the temple to court dishonour by feigned madness, and finally seek the end of suffering in caves and cremation grounds through constant remembrance of Rudra and his grace. The Gaṇakārikā and its Ratnaṭīkā systematise the path as eight pentads and one triad.

## Distinctive positions
- Five categories (pañcārtha) sum up the teaching: effect (kārya), cause (kāraṇa), union (yoga), rule (vidhi) and the end of suffering (duḥkhānta) (PABh on PS 1.1; reported by Śaṅkara at BSBh 2.2.37).
- The Lord (pati) is the cause who produces the whole effect - knowledge-qualities (vidyā), the insentient 'parts' (kalā) and the souls (paśu) - at will, all at once or in sequence, depending neither on karma nor on the soul (PABh on PS 2.5-6).
- All sentient beings except the Lord are paśus, including the gods from Brahmā down and those liberated through Sāṃkhya-Yoga (PABh on PS 1.1).
- The end of suffering is reached by the Lord's grace (īśaprasāda, PS 5.40), not by knowledge, dispassion, merit, powers or renunciation alone (PABh on PS 1.1).
- The end of suffering is twofold: the absolute cessation of all suffering, and the attainment of the Lord's powers of knowing and acting (Sarvadarśanasaṃgraha ch. 6).
- Yoga is the union of the self with the Lord through the mind (PABh on PS 1.1, 1.20).
- The observance is 'beyond the āśramas' (atyāśrama) and open only to brahmin men (PABh on PS 1.1, 4.20).
- Seeking dishonour is the highest austerity: the reviled ascetic's demerit passes to those who revile him and their merit passes to him (PS 3.6-9, 3.19, 4.9).
- By this rule a brahmin who reaches Rudra's proximity does not return (PS 4.19-20).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence high

**Names:** Paśupati, Pati, Rudra, Maheśvara / Mahādeva, Śaṅkara, Īśāna
**Descriptions:** Pati: he 'reaches' and 'protects' the paśus by an infinite power of knowledge and by his lordship (PABh on PS 1.1).; Kāraṇa: by will (kāmitva) he produces the whole effect - vidyā, kalā and paśu - all at once or in sequence as he wishes (PABh on PS 2.5-6).; His activity is play (krīḍā): he produces, favours and conceals the threefold effect (PABh on PS 1.9).; Īśāna of all knowledges, lord of all beings, overlord of Brahman, Brahmā; 'may Śiva be mine, always Śiva' (PS 5.42-47, the Īśāna mantra).; Śaṅkara, maker of all happiness and nirvāṇa (PABh on PS 2.20).
**Negations:** he does not depend on karma or on the soul (na karmāpekṣa, PABh on PS 2.6); he alone is not a paśu (PABh on PS 1.1)
**Relation to self:** The paśus are all sentient beings other than the Lord, even the gods from Brahmā down and those liberated by Sāṃkhya-Yoga; their bondage is lack of lordship (anaiśvarya). By the rule of practice and the Lord's grace the soul reaches Rudra's proximity (PS 4.19) and union (sāyujya, PS 5.33) and gains his powers of knowing and acting; the Lord alone remains the independent cause.
**Relation to world:** The effect - the insentient kalās (elements, qualities, organs) and the souls - is eternal yet wholly dependent on the Lord's will; he emits, maintains, withdraws and favours it.
**Caveat:** Pāśupata theism keeps the Lord and the soul distinct even in liberation and makes the Lord only the (efficient) cause; it would reject both an impersonal absolute in which the soul dissolves and the Vedāntin claim (BS 2.2.37) that the Lord must also be the material cause.
