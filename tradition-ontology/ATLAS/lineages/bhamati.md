The line of post-Śaṅkara Advaita founded on Vācaspati Miśra's Bhāmatī, drawing on Maṇḍana Miśra, continued by Amalānanda's Kalpataru and Appayya Dīkṣita's Parimala. It places ignorance in the individual selves, explains the jīva by limitation, admits many jīvas, and holds that the mind, perfected by contemplation, turns the verbal knowledge of the mahāvākya into direct realization.

## Distinctive positions
- Ignorance resides in the individual selves (jīvāśrita) and has Brahman as its object; the mutual dependence of jīva and avidyā is beginningless like seed and sprout.
- Avidyās are many, one for each jīva; hence many jīvas (aneka-jīva).
- Limitation theory (avaccheda-vāda): the jīva is Brahman as delimited by the inner organ, as space is delimited by a pot.
- The Upaniṣadic sentence yields mediate (parokṣa) knowledge; the mind purified by sustained contemplation is the instrument of direct realization.
- Contemplation (nididhyāsana, prasaṅkhyāna) is the principal means; hearing and reflection are auxiliaries.
- Vedic works produce the desire to know (vividiṣā), not knowledge itself.

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Brahman
**Descriptions:** immeasurable bliss and knowledge, deathless, whose apparent transformations the elements are, through two indescribable ignorances (Bhāmatī, opening verse)
**Negations:** as in Advaita generally
**Relation to self:** The jīva is Brahman as delimited (avacchinna) by the inner organ; ignorance resides in the jīvas, not in pure Brahman.
**Relation to world:** The world is Brahman's apparent transformation (vivarta) through the avidyās of the jīvas, with Brahman as its object.
**Caveat:** Shares Advaita's ultimate; differs only in the theory of the locus of ignorance and of the jīva. Partisans hold this theory to be correct.
