The Pāṇinian grammarians as a school of philosophy of language. From Patañjali's question 'what is the word?' and Kātyāyana's doctrine of the eternal word, Bhartṛhari's Vākyapadīya built a monism of the Word: Brahman is the Word-principle that appears as the world, all cognition is word-permeated, meaning is borne by the indivisible sphoṭa and grasped as pratibhā, and grammar itself is a path to liberation. Maṇḍana Miśra defended sphoṭa; Helārāja, Puṇyarāja and Kaiyaṭa commented; Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita, Kauṇḍa Bhaṭṭa and Nāgeśa systematized the 'new' grammatical philosophy. Opposed by Mīmāṃsā, Nyāya, Buddhists, Jains and (on paśyantī) the Kashmir Śaivas.

## Distinctive positions
- Brahman, without beginning or end, is the principle of the Word (śabdatattva), the imperishable, which appears (vivartate) as things; from it the world proceeds (VP 1.1).
- The one Brahman appears as many through its powers, of which time (kāla-śakti) is the chief (VP 1.2–3).
- There is no cognition in the world not interpenetrated by the word; all knowledge shines as if pierced by the word (VP 1.123 Iyer).
- The meaning-bearer is the sphoṭa, a partless, sequence-less word manifested by the sequential sounds (dhvani/nāda); the sentence (vākya-sphoṭa) is the real unit and word-meanings are abstractions from it (apoddhāra).
- The meaning of a sentence is grasped as a flash of intuition (pratibhā), which even guides animals' instinctive action (VP 2.143–152).
- Speech has three levels — vaikharī (articulated), madhyamā (mental, sequential), paśyantī (undivided, 'seeing'); the highest is the Veda's supreme station (VP 1.142 Iyer).
- Grammar is the door to liberation, the remedy for the impurities of speech, the straight royal road to the highest (VP 1.14–16); the purified use of words is a yoga of the word (śabdapūrva-yoga).
- Word, meaning and their relation are eternal (siddhe śabdārthasambandhe — Kātyāyana's first vārttika); grammar only restricts correct usage for the sake of merit (dharma-niyama).
- Reasoning without scripture is unstable: what is inferred with effort by the skilled is explained otherwise by the more skilled (VP 1.34).
- Every word ultimately denotes Being (sattā), the great universal, which is Brahman (VP 3.1, 3.2).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Śabda-brahman / śabdatattva (the Word-principle), akṣara (the imperishable), paśyantī, the supreme station of speech, Sattā / mahāsattā (Being, the great universal)
**Descriptions:** without beginning or end, the principle of the Word, imperishable, appearing as things (VP 1.1); one, appearing as many through its powers, though not separate from them (VP 1.2); the single seed of all, existing as enjoyer, enjoyed and enjoyment (VP 1.4); the Being that all words finally denote (VP 3.1–3.2)
**Negations:** paśyantī is undivided, with all sequence withdrawn (Vṛtti on VP 1.142); not established by reasoning alone (VP 1.30, 1.34)
**Relation to self:** Consciousness is of the nature of speech: if the eternal word-nature of awareness departed, light would not illumine (VP 1.124 Iyer); the Word is the inner consciousness of beings.
**Relation to world:** The world is an appearance (vivarta) of the Word-principle, proceeding through its time-power; the universe evolved first from the Vedic metres (VP 1.1, 1.3, 1.120 Iyer).
**Caveat:** The grammarians hold a single ultimate but not a personal Lord. Their identification of the Word (paśyantī) with Brahman is rejected by the Kashmir Śaivas (who place parā vāk above paśyantī), by Buddhist and Jain critics, and by Mīmāṃsā (which denies sphoṭa); whether Bhartṛhari's 'vivarta' means illusory appearance in the later Advaita sense is disputed by scholars.
