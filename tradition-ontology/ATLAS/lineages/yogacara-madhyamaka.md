**Status:** absorbed

The 8th-century synthesis in which Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla combined Madhyamaka's emptiness (shown chiefly by the 'neither one nor many' argument), Yogācāra's mind-only as the description of conventional reality, and Dharmakīrti's epistemology; their encyclopaedic Tattvasaṅgraha and Pañjikā examine and reject the positions of every Indian school. Kamalaśīla's Bhāvanākramas set out the gradual path of compassion, bodhicitta, calm and insight. Tibetan doxography classifies the line as Yogācāra-Svātantrika, sub-divided into true-aspectarian and false-aspectarian branches.

## Distinctive positions
- Nothing that is held to be real by ourselves or others is truly one or truly many; therefore it lacks own-nature, like a reflection (MAL 1).
- Conventionally all appearances are mind only; ultimately even mind is without own-nature — relying on mind-only one understands the absence of external things, relying on Madhyamaka one understands that even that mind lacks self (MAL 92–93).
- Consciousness is by nature self-illuminating (svasaṃvedana) at the conventional level (MAL 16–17).
- Riding the two chariots of Yogācāra and Madhyamaka and holding the reins of reasoning, one is a true Mahāyānist (MAL 93, the tradition's summary).
- Meditation must be preceded by correct analysis (bhūtapratyavekṣā); mere non-thought is not non-conceptual wisdom (Bhāvanākrama III).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** niḥsvabhāvatā established by the absence of true unity and plurality, paramārtha-satya, śūnyatā, nirvikalpa-jñāna's object: freedom from all elaboration
**Descriptions:** the absence of own-nature in all things, shown because nothing is truly one or truly many (MAL 1); the ultimate is free from all elaborations; the nominal ultimate (the negation of arising) accords with it (MAL 70–72); that which the yogin enters when analysing he finds no dharma arisen in the three times: non-conceptual wisdom pacifying all elaboration (Bhāvanākrama III)
**Negations:** neither one nor many (ekānekaviyoga); neither external objects nor mind are ultimately real (MAL 92–93)
**Relation to self:** Persons and dharmas alike are without own-nature; conventionally there is only self-illuminating mind.
**Relation to world:** The world of appearances is conventionally mind-only and 'satisfying only when not analysed'; ultimately it is empty.
**Caveat:** Mind-only is accepted only conventionally, as a step; neither mind nor emptiness is posited as a positive ultimate entity.
