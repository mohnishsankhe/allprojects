The teaching and community of the Buddha Gotama and his first generations of disciples as preserved in the Pali Nikāyas and Vinaya (with parallels in the Chinese Āgamas and the other early schools' Vinayas): the four noble truths, not-self, dependent origination, kamma and rebirth, and a gradual path of virtue, meditative concentration and wisdom leading to nibbāna in this life. As a label this is an umbrella for what all the early schools share; the tradition itself speaks of 'the Dhamma and Vinaya' (dhammavinaya) taught by the Buddha.

## Distinctive positions
- A middle way that avoids both devotion to sensual pleasure and self-mortification (SN 56.11).
- Four noble truths: suffering, its origin in craving, its cessation, and the noble eightfold path leading to it (SN 56.11).
- The five aggregates are impermanent, suffering and not-self: 'this is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self' (SN 22.59).
- Dependent origination: 'when this exists, that comes to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases' (SN 12.21, MN 38); whoever sees dependent origination sees the Dhamma (MN 28).
- Kamma is intention (cetanā) (AN 6.63); beings are owners and heirs of their deeds (MN 135).
- Nibbāna — the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion (SN 38.1), the deathless, the unconditioned — is to be realized in this very life.
- The gradual training of virtue, concentration and wisdom (MN 27, DN 2; AN 3.88) culminating in the knowledge of the destruction of the effluents.
- Certain questions (whether the world is eternal or finite, whether the soul is the same as the body, whether the Tathāgata exists after death) are set aside as not conducive to liberation (MN 63, MN 72).
- Rejection of the authority of the Veda and of purity by birth: one becomes a brahmin by deeds, not birth (Snp 3.9, MN 93, DN 27).
- After the Teacher's death the Dhamma and Vinaya he taught are to be the teacher; the disciple is to be an island and refuge to himself, with the Dhamma as island (DN 16).
- A fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen (DN 16); women can realize the fruits of the path from stream-entry to arahantship (Cullavagga 10.1).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence high

**Names:** nibbāna, the deathless (amata), the unconditioned (asaṅkhata), the unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned (ajāta, abhūta, akata, asaṅkhata), that sphere (tad āyatanaṃ), the element of nibbāna with and without residue (saupādisesā / anupādisesā nibbānadhātu), the further shore, the island, the refuge, the peaceful, the sublime (SN 43 synonyms)
**Descriptions:** the destruction of greed, the destruction of hatred, the destruction of delusion (SN 38.1); the cessation of becoming (bhavanirodha) (SN 12.68); the stilling of all formations, the relinquishing of all acquisitions, the destruction of craving, dispassion, cessation (MN 26; AN 3.32); the highest happiness (Dhp 203–204); the deathless state (Dhp 21); the unshakeable liberation of mind — 'unshakeable is my release; this is the last birth' (SN 56.11)
**Negations:** there is that sphere where there is no earth, water, fire or air, no sphere of infinite space … no neither-perception-nor-non-perception, neither this world nor another, neither sun nor moon; no coming, going, staying, passing away or arising; without support, without occurrence, without object — this is the end of suffering (Ud 8.1); there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned; were there not, no escape from the born, become, made, conditioned would be known (Ud 8.3); consciousness that does not manifest (viññāṇaṃ anidassanaṃ), infinite, radiant all round, where earth, water, fire and air find no footing (DN 11; MN 49); the Tathāgata released from reckoning by form … is deep, immeasurable, hard to fathom like the ocean; 'is reborn', 'is not reborn', 'both', 'neither' do not apply (MN 72)
**Relation to self:** Not a self and not the self's true nature: all conditioned things are impermanent and suffering, and 'all phenomena (sabbe dhammā) are not-self' (Dhp 277–279; AN 3.136). The ordinary person 'conceives nibbāna as mine, delights in nibbāna', the Tathāgata does not (MN 1).
**Relation to world:** Not a cause, ground or source of the world: the world (loka), its origin, its cessation and the way to its cessation are declared 'in this fathom-long body with its perception and mind' (SN 2.26; AN 4.45). Nibbāna is the cessation of the world of the six senses, not what the world is made of.
**Caveat:** The suttas speak of one unconditioned element (asaṅkhatā dhātu), but deny that it is a self, a creator, a Brahman or a metaphysical ground from which beings or the world proceed: the Brahmajāla (DN 1) lists the views that identify a permanent self or world as views born of contact and craving, and MN 1 criticises 'conceiving' even nibbāna. Any reading of nibbāna as 'the one reality called by many names' is an interpretation the tradition itself would reject if it implies a substantial essence; it accepts only that the same unconditioned is named by many epithets (SN 43).
