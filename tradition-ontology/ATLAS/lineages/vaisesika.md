**Status:** absorbed

Vaiśeṣika is the Vedic darśana of categorial analysis and atomism founded on Kaṇāda's Vaiśeṣika Sūtra and systematized by Praśastapāda. It explains dharma as the source of prosperity and the highest good, and holds that knowledge of the truth of the categories — through their similarities and dissimilarities — produced by dharma leads to liberation. Its commentators (Vyomaśiva, Śrīdhara, Udayana) made it theistic and joined it to Nyāya; after the 11th c. it survives mainly within syncretic Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika.

## Distinctive positions
- Dharma is that from which prosperity (abhyudaya) and the highest good (niḥśreyasa) result (VS 1.1.2); the authority of the Veda rests on its being 'the statement of that' (VS 1.1.3).
- Reality is analysed into categories (padārtha): substance, quality, motion, universal, particularity and inherence — six in Praśastapāda and the Upaskāra recension of the sūtras — with absence added as a seventh in later works (Udayana's Lakṣaṇāvalī, Śivāditya's Saptapadārthī).
- Nine substances: earth, water, fire, air, ākāśa, time, space, self and mind (VS 1.1.4).
- Twenty-four qualities (Praśastapāda), seventeen named in the sūtra (VS 1.1.5) and seven more included by its word 'ca'; five kinds of motion (VS 1.1.6).
- Earth, water, fire and air consist of eternal, partless, spherical atoms (paramāṇu) that combine into dyads and larger wholes; what exists without a cause is eternal (VS 4.1.1, 7.1.26).
- Ultimate particularity (viśeṣa) distinguishes each eternal substance — traditionally connected with the school's name.
- Only two means of knowledge: perception and inference; verbal knowledge is explained as inference (VS 9.18–19 in Candrānanda's recension; Praśastapāda).
- Motions without visible cause — the needle's approach to a magnet, the ascent of sap, fire's upward flame, the first motion of atoms and minds, and the mind's departure at death and entry at birth — are caused by the unseen (adṛṣṭa) (VS 5.1.15, 5.2.8, 5.2.14, 5.2.19).
- Yoga is the state in which, the mind resting in the self, the embodied one has neither pleasure nor pain; liberation is the absence of contact with a body and the non-arising of a new one when adṛṣṭa is absent (VS 5.2.17, 5.2.20).
- The world is periodically dissolved and recreated by the will of Maheśvara acting through the adṛṣṭa of selves (Praśastapāda).
- Heat transforms qualities at the level of atoms (pīlupāka), against Nyāya's view that the whole is baked (piṭharapāka).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Maheśvara, Īśvara, niḥśreyasa (the highest good, as goal)
**Descriptions:** Saluted as 'Īśvara, the cause' at the opening of the Padārthadharmasaṅgraha; the knowledge that leads to the highest good arises from dharma manifested by His command.; By His will the world is dissolved at the end of a Brahmā's lifespan and created again for the selves' experience, through their adṛṣṭa (Praśastapāda).; The highest good (niḥśreyasa) is the fruit of dharma (VS 1.1.2): liberation, in which the self has no contact with a body (VS 5.2.20).
**Negations:** the Vaiśeṣika Sūtra itself does not name Īśvara
**Relation to self:** The Lord is a self distinct from the innumerable individual selves, each made distinct by its own ultimate particularity (viśeṣa).
**Relation to world:** Efficient cause who initiates the motion of eternal atoms at creation and their separation at dissolution; the nine substances and the other categories are real.
**Caveat:** Early Vaiśeṣika (the sūtras) does not explicitly name a Lord (scholarly account); theism appears from Praśastapāda on. The school would reject monism: innumerable eternal atoms, selves and minds, each individuated by ultimate particularity, are irreducibly many — the school's very name is traditionally linked with 'particularity'.
