The school of Vedic exegesis founded on Jaimini's Mīmāṃsā Sūtra: it inquires into dharma as known from Vedic injunctions and develops a system of rules for interpreting the Veda (the adhikaraṇa method), a realist epistemology built around the intrinsic validity of cognition and the authorless, eternal Veda, and a theory of ritual action and its unseen result (apūrva). After Śabara's commentary it divided into the schools of Kumārila (Bhāṭṭa) and Prabhākara (Prābhākara), with Murāri Miśra's short-lived 'third path'.

## Distinctive positions
- Dharma is a beneficial object known only through Vedic injunction (codanā); perception and reasoning based on it cannot know dharma (MS 1.1.2, 1.1.4).
- The relation of word and meaning is original (autpattika), not made by any person; hence the Veda's injunctions are a self-sufficient means of knowledge (MS 1.1.5).
- Words (letters) are eternal; utterance only manifests them (MS 1.1.6–23).
- The Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya) and beginningless; names attached to its recensions derive from their expounders (MS 1.1.27–32).
- Every cognition is valid of itself; invalidity is established only by a defect in its cause or a later contradicting cognition (svataḥ-prāmāṇya).
- The Veda consists of injunction, mantra, name, prohibition and explanatory praise; all of it is meaningful through connection with injunctions (MS 1.2.7).
- A ritual act, though perishable, produces an unseen potency (apūrva) that yields the promised result later (MS 2.1.5 with Śabara).
- The sacrificial act, not the deity, is principal; the deity is that to which the offering is directed as named in the injunction or mantra (MS 9.1.9).
- No creator god and no total creation or dissolution of the world are established by any means of knowledge (Kumārila; Śālikanātha).
- No person — human, yogin or god — perceives dharma directly; claims of omniscience in matters of dharma are rejected (Kumārila).
- Heaven (svarga) is the original declared goal of Vedic rites; later teachers add liberation (mokṣa) as the ending of embodiment through the exhaustion of karma.

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence high

**Names:** dharma, the Veda (authorless, eternal word), niḥśreyasa (the highest good to which dharma joins a person)
**Descriptions:** Dharma is the beneficial object (artha) whose mark is Vedic injunction (MS 1.1.2).; Injunction alone can make known what is past, present, future, subtle, hidden or remote; no sense faculty can (Śabara on MS 1.1.2).; The Veda is authorless and eternal, its word-meaning relation original, and it is therefore a self-sufficient means of knowing dharma (MS 1.1.5, 1.1.27–32).
**Negations:** No creator who composes the Veda or dispenses the fruits of action is established (ŚV sambandhākṣepaparihāra 42–116; Prakaraṇapañcikā ch. 7).; Deities are not the principal element of a rite, nor embodied eaters of offerings (MS 9.1.9 with Śabara).; No person perceives dharma directly (ŚV codanā 110–136).
**Relation to self:** Selves are many, eternal and all-pervading; each is agent and enjoyer of the fruits of its own ritual action.
**Relation to world:** The world is real and beginningless, with no total creation or dissolution; it is the field in which dharma and adharma ripen.
**Caveat:** Classical Mīmāṃsā posits no single ultimate in the Vedāntic sense: no Brahman as the one ground of all and no creator Īśvara. Its highest authority is the authorless Veda, its highest object dharma, whose declared fruit is heaven (svarga) and, for later teachers, release as the end of embodiment. Reading dharma or the Veda as a name of 'the One' of RV 1.164.46 is an interpretation the classical school does not make. Later theistic Mīmāṃsakas (Āpadeva, Laugākṣi Bhāskara, Vedānta Deśika) dedicate dharma to Govinda/Īśvara, but still do not make the Lord the subject matter of the Pūrva Mīmāṃsā.
