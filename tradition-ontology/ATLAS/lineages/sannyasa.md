The ideal of the wandering renouncer (saṃnyāsin, parivrājaka, yati) as set out in the Saṃnyāsa Upaniṣads: who may renounce and when, the rite that makes the renouncer ritually dead to the world, the kinds of renouncer and their marks, rules of alms, dwelling and conduct, and the claim that true renunciation is knowledge of the Self. Living orders (Daśanāmī, Vaiṣṇava tridaṇḍins and others) are treated under their own lineages; this entry is the textual ideal.

## Distinctive positions
- One may renounce 'on the very day one becomes dispassionate', from any stage of life (Jābāla 4).
- True renunciation is the union of the individual and supreme Self, not merely abandoning rites or uttering the formula (Maitreya 2.17).
- The outer topknot and sacred thread are replaced by inner ones: knowledge is the topknot, the Self or praṇava the thread (Jābāla 5; Brahma; Paramahaṃsa; Parabrahma).
- Renunciation is spoken of as fourfold (from dispassion, knowledge, both, ritual) and renouncers are of four or six kinds — kuṭīcaka, bahūdaka, haṃsa, paramahaṃsa, turīyātīta, avadhūta — with rising freedom from marks.
- The staff of knowledge, not the wooden staff, makes the ekadaṇḍin (Paramahaṃsa); a Vaiṣṇava strand instead requires the triple staff, topknot and thread for life (Śāṭyāyanīya).
- Renunciation is a ritual death: the renouncer performs his own śrāddha, places the fires in himself, gives fearlessness to all beings and leaves the ritual world.
- The paramahaṃsa and avadhūta live with unmanifest marks and conduct, beyond castes and stages, 'not mad but acting mad' (Jābāla 6; Avadhūta 2).
- The renouncer takes no disciples for gain, keeps no books, gives no lectures and avoids householders (Nāradaparivrājaka 5; Sannyāsa 2.79–85) — though the Avadhūta allows action for the world's good.
- Alms are taken in the bee's way (mādhukarī) from many houses; food from a single house is forbidden.
- Last thought and kind of renunciation determine the world attained; the turīyātīta and avadhūta attain kaivalya.

## The ultimate in this lineage
`sourced` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Brahman, Ātman (the Self), Nārāyaṇa / Viṣṇu, Śiva (the jīva is Śiva), turīya / turīyātīta, the praṇava beyond its measures
**Descriptions:** The Self realized by the renouncer as 'so'ham' — 'I am He' (Maitreya 2.1; Nāradaparivrājaka 9).; The sixteenth measure of the praṇava and the state beyond the fourth (Nāradaparivrājaka 8; Paramahaṃsaparivrājaka).; That in which renunciation consists: the union of jīva and the supreme (Maitreya 2.17).
**Negations:** neti neti (Nāradaparivrājaka 9, quoting the Bṛhadāraṇyaka); not reached by staff, shaving, garb or show (Nāradaparivrājaka 5)
**Relation to self:** Identical: the renouncer's own Self is Brahman, and the jīva in the body-temple is Śiva alone (Maitreya 2.1; Avadhūta 1). In the Vaiṣṇava Śāṭyāyanīya the renouncer serves Viṣṇu and bears his marks.
**Relation to world:** The world is renounced as the field of the three desires (for wife, wealth and worlds); for the knower it is superimposed and no longer binding; rites cease because 'the sun of consciousness neither rises nor sets' (Maitreya 2.14).
**Caveat:** The renunciate ideal is a way of life before it is a doctrine: the texts insist that the ultimate is realized only by one who has actually abandoned the ritual world, and they disagree among themselves (Advaitin paramahaṃsa vs Vaiṣṇava tridaṇḍin) on whether outer marks are to be kept. A one-truth reading must not dissolve that insistence on renunciation or the Vaiṣṇava strand's theism.
