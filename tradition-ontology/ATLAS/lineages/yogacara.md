The 'practice of yoga' school of Indian Mahāyāna, founded (in its own account) on the revelation of the bodhisattva Maitreya to Asaṅga and systematized by Asaṅga and Vasubandhu. It teaches that the world as experienced is the unfolding of cognition from the seeds in the store-consciousness, analyses experience through the three natures, and maps the path from calm and insight to the transformation of the basis and buddhahood with its four wisdoms and three bodies.

## Distinctive positions
- The three realms are cognition-only (vijñaptimātra): what appears as an external object is cognition appearing as an object (Viṃśatikā 1; Triṃśikā 17).
- Eight kinds of consciousness: six operative consciousnesses, the afflicted mind (kliṣṭa-manas) and the store-consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna) holding all seeds (Triṃśikā 2–16; Mahāyānasaṃgraha ch. 1).
- Three natures (trisvabhāva): the imagined, the dependent and the perfected; and the three naturelessnesses by which the Prajñāpāramitā's 'all dharmas are without own-being' is to be understood (Saṃdhinirmocana ch. 6–7; Triṃśikā 20–25).
- Unreal imagination (abhūtaparikalpa) exists; the duality of grasped and grasper does not; emptiness exists in it (Madhyāntavibhāga 1.2).
- Liberation is the transformation of the basis (āśraya-parāvṛtti), the store-consciousness turning into mirror-like wisdom and the other consciousnesses into the other three wisdoms.
- Beings belong to different spiritual lineages (gotra), some with no lineage for liberation (Bodhisattvabhūmi; Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra ch. 3) — a thesis later Mahāyāna authors contested.
- The yogin's path of calm and insight is mapped in detail: nine stages of settling the mind, seven attentions, four kinds of meditation object (Śrāvakabhūmi).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence high

**Names:** pariniṣpanna-svabhāva (the perfected nature), tathatā (suchness), dharmadhātu, vijñaptimātratā (cognition-only-ness), śūnyatā (emptiness of duality), dharmatā, anāsrava-dhātu (the undefiled element)
**Descriptions:** the dependent's constant absence of the imagined (Triṃśikā 21); the ultimate of dharmas, always thus, cognition-only-ness itself (Triṃśikā 25); the non-existence of duality and the existence of that non-existence (Madhyāntavibhāga 1.14); inconceivable, wholesome, constant, blissful — the liberation-body, the dharma-body (Triṃśikā 30); undifferentiated in all beings; purified, it is tathāgatahood (Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra 9.37)
**Negations:** neither existent nor non-existent, neither thus nor otherwise, neither born nor perishing (Mahāyānasūtrālaṃkāra 6.1); neither other nor not other than the dependent (Triṃśikā 22); inexpressible (Bodhisattvabhūmi, tattvārtha)
**Relation to self:** There is no self; the afflicted mind mistakes the store-consciousness for one. The ultimate is realized when the grasping of self and dharmas ceases and the basis is transformed.
**Relation to world:** The world is the transformation of consciousness (cognition-only); the perfected nature is that world's true nature, reached when its imagined duality is seen through.
**Caveat:** Yogācāra denies any self or creator; suchness is not a substance or a Brahman, and cognition-only is not a cosmic mind but the mind-streams of beings. The school rejects the Madhyamaka reading that even the dependent and the perfected are simply empty, and it teaches differing lineages rather than one destiny for all — reading it as 'one truth under many names' must keep these denials.
