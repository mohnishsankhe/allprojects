# Buddhist epistemology and logic (pramāṇa school)

**Regions:** South India and Kāñcī (tradition's account of Dignāga), Nālandā, Kashmir, Vikramaśīla, Jagaddala, Tibet (tshad ma curriculum), China and Japan (yinming)
**Dates:** Scholarly account: Dignāga c. 480–540; Dharmakīrti c. 6th–7th c.; the school active in India until the late 12th c. and continued in Tibet; (confidence moderate)
**Status:** living

The Buddhist tradition of logic and epistemology founded by Dignāga and recast by Dharmakīrti, which recognized only perception and inference, grounded all reasoning in causal efficacy and momentary particulars, explained language by exclusion, and used this apparatus both to defend the Buddha's authority and liberation and to refute self, God and the authority of the Veda. Its Indian line ended with the monasteries of Bengal and Bihar; it lives on in the Tibetan monastic curriculum.

## Distinctive positions
- There are exactly two means of valid cognition — perception and inference — because there are exactly two kinds of object, the unique particular and the universal (PS 1.2; NB 1.2–3, 1.12–17).
- Perception is free of conceptual construction and non-erroneous (NB 1.4); it has four kinds: sensory, mental, reflexive self-awareness and yogic (NB 1.7–11).
- A valid reason has three characteristics (trairūpya): present in the subject, present only in similar cases, absent from all dissimilar cases (NB 2.5); there are three kinds of valid reason — effect, own-nature and non-perception (NB 2.11).
- Words and concepts do not reach real universals; they operate by exclusion of the other (anyāpoha).
- Whatever exists is momentary, since to exist is to be causally efficacious (Ratnakīrti: yat sat tat kṣaṇikam).
- Every cognition is reflexively aware of itself (svasaṃvedana); the result of a means of knowledge is this self-awareness.
- The Buddha is a 'person of valid cognition' (pramāṇabhūta) because he knows what is to be abandoned and adopted with its means, a knowledge born of compassion cultivated over many lives (PV Pramāṇasiddhi chapter).
- No creator god, no authorless scripture, no permanent self: each is refuted by reasoning.

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence high

**Names:** paramārtha-sat (the ultimately real: the causally efficacious particular), svalakṣaṇa (the unique particular), non-dual cognition (at the cognition-only level), prabhāsvara citta (the luminous mind), śūnyatā / nairātmya (whose seeing liberates)
**Descriptions:** what is capable of causal efficacy is ultimately real (PV 3.3 conventional; NB 1.14–15); cognition in itself is undivided; grasped and grasper are seen only by distorted vision (PV 3.354 conventional); the mind is luminous by nature; the stains are adventitious (PV 2.208 conventional); liberation comes from seeing emptiness (PV 2.253 conventional)
**Negations:** no permanent means of knowledge, no creator God, no eternal self, no authorless Veda (PV Pramāṇasiddhi); the particular is inexpressible; universals are not real
**Relation to self:** There is no self; seeing a self is the root of attachment and all faults; seeing selflessness uproots it.
**Relation to world:** The world of experience consists of momentary particulars; at the higher level it is cognition without real division into subject and object.
**Caveat:** The pramāṇa school does not posit a single ultimate being: its 'ultimately real' is a plurality of momentary particulars, and at the cognition-only level non-dual cognition, not a universal consciousness; it explicitly refutes God and any permanent reality. The one-truth reading must record this denial.
