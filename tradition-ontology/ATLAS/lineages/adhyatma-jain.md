**Regions:** Agra, Jaunpur, Sanganer and Jaipur, Delhi, north India (Braj, Rajasthan)
**Dates:** Scholarly account: from c. 1620s (Banārsīdās's circle in Agra) into the 18th c. (Jaipur, Ṭoḍarmal); (confidence moderate)
**Status:** living

A seventeenth-century lay mystical movement of north-Indian Jains centred on Banārsīdās of Agra (1587–c. 1643). Its members, many of them Śvetāmbara-born merchants, studied the Digambara Kundakunda's Samayasāra with Amṛtacandra's commentary in vernacular circles and taught the priority of the soul's own knowing nature over ritual. The tradition's account links it to the Digambara Terāpantha reform and to Ṭoḍarmal of Jaipur; the scholarly account sees the same line of descent but dates the Terāpantha's formal emergence to the later 17th–18th c. The broader Śvetāmbara 'adhyātma' writing of Ānandaghana, Yaśovijaya and Devacandra is a parallel current, not part of this circle (Yaśovijaya criticised it).

## Distinctive positions
- The soul's own pure nature, known by the ultimate standpoint (niścaya-naya) and by direct self-experience (anubhava), is the heart of the Jina's teaching; ritual and outer conduct belong to the conventional standpoint (vyavahāra).
- Lay study circles (śailī) read the Samayasāra and its commentaries in Hindi/Braj rather than relying on monks or bhaṭṭārakas.
- Critique of ritual formalism, of the authority of the Digambara bhaṭṭārakas and (by its Śvetāmbara-born members) of Śvetāmbara monastic claims; its critics say it rejected image worship with flowers and similar rites.
- After an early one-sided emphasis on niścaya (described by Banārsīdās himself as a fall into disregard of conduct), the mature position holds both standpoints together, with the fourteen guṇasthānas as the map of progress (Samayasāra Nāṭaka's closing chapter).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** śuddhātman (the pure self), samayasāra (the essence of the self), paramātman, cidānanda (consciousness-bliss), jñāyaka-bhāva (the knower)
**Descriptions:** The self shining by self-experience, whose nature is consciousness, cutting through all other states (Samayasāra-kalaśa 1).; One, pure, made of perception and knowledge, formless; not even an atom of anything else is mine (Samayasāra 38).; Who the supreme self is, that am I (Yogīndu, Yogasāra 22).
**Negations:** Not the body, not karma, not the passions (Iṣṭopadeśa 50).; Not the doer of material karma in the ultimate standpoint.
**Relation to self:** Identical with one's own pure self as seen by the ultimate standpoint; realised through discriminative knowledge and self-experience.
**Relation to world:** The world of substances is real; the self is its knower, not its maker; conventional conduct belongs to the path, not to the self's nature.
**Caveat:** 'I am the supreme self' in the Adhyātma texts means that each soul's own nature is the perfected state, not that all souls are one Brahman; the movement accepts the Jain plurality of souls and the reality of the world, and its mature form insists on holding the conventional standpoint alongside the ultimate.
