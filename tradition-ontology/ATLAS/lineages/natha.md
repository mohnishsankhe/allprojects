**Regions:** Gorakhpur and eastern Uttar Pradesh, Punjab (Gorakh Ṭillā / Tilla Jogian), Rajasthan (Jodhpur), Haryana (Asthal Bohar), Gujarat and Kutch (Dhinodhar, Girnar), Nepal (Gorkha, Kathmandu valley), Bengal and Assam (Kāmarūpa), Maharashtra (Tryambakeśvar, Madhi), coastal Karnataka (Kadri, Mangaluru), Andhra (Telugu Nāth legends)
**Dates:** Tradition's account: Ādinātha (Śiva) is the first Nāth; Matsyendra received the teaching from him; Gorakṣa is held to be immortal and to have appeared in each of the four ages; Scholarly account: Matsyendra c. 9th–10th c. (honoured by Abhinavagupta, Tantrāloka 1.7); Gorakṣa c. 11th–13th c.; the order with its twelve panths is consolidated later (c. 15th–18th c.); (confidence moderate)
**Status:** living

The Nāth tradition traces itself to Ādinātha (Śiva), Matsyendranātha and Gorakṣanātha and to the nine Nāths and eighty-four Siddhas. It joins the Kaula teaching of Matsyendra's Yoginīkaula with the body-centred yoga later called haṭha, aiming at sahaja, the samarasa of Śiva and Śakti, liberation while living and the perfected, deathless body. Its ascetics (Kānphaṭa yogīs) are organised in twelve panths with monasteries from Gorakhpur and Tilla Jogian to Kadri and Nepal; its vernacular sayings (Gorakh Bānī) and sagas (Gopīcand, Bharthari, Mayanāmatī, the rescue of Matsyendra) shaped Sant, Vārkarī and folk religion across India.

## Distinctive positions
- The body (piṇḍa) contains the whole cosmos (brahmāṇḍa); liberation is realised in and through the body.
- The goal is sahaja-samādhi / samarasa: the mind seeing the mind, the stilling of breath and mind in the void (śūnya) beyond the pairs (AmŚ 54; GŚ 94).
- Perfection of the body (kāya-siddhi): an adamantine, deathless siddha body; conquest of death and time (kāla-vañcana) through retaining bindu and the moon's nectar.
- Reversal (ulṭā sādhana): turning the downward flows of breath, bindu and mind upward to the head.
- The guru is indispensable; the teaching passes ear to ear and is kept secret from the untested.
- Rejection of caste, image-worship, pilgrimage, fasting and Vedic ritual as means to liberation for the knower (Akulavīra A.43–46; Gorakh Bānī).
- Moderation and restraint (saṃyam), the 'middle way' of the yogī (Gorakh Bānī).
- Distinctive order: split-ear initiation (kān-phaṛāī), earrings (darśan / mudrā), the nād horn on the selī thread, the dhūnī fire, the greeting 'ādeś', burial (samādhi) of yogīs rather than cremation.
- Siddhis are signs, not the goal; the knower does not grasp them (Akulavīra B.83–84).

## The ultimate in this lineage
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Names:** Alakh Nirañjan, Nirañjana, Param pad (the supreme state), Śiva / Ādinātha, Śūnya (the void), Sahaja
**Descriptions:** stainless, motionless, eternal, actionless, attributeless, great, sky-like knowledge and bliss (GŚ 99); the samarasa of body and supreme state (SSP 5); the state where the mind sees the mind in sahaja-samādhi (AmŚ 54)
**Negations:** fearless, supportless, without elaboration, without resting-place, formless (GŚ 92); neither temple nor mosque (Gorakh Bānī)
**Relation to self:** The self is Śiva; the yogī enters the supreme 'like milk into milk' and becomes non-dual (GŚ 100); the guru's grace unites body and pada.
**Relation to world:** The whole cosmos is within the body (piṇḍa-brahmāṇḍa); the body is to be perfected, not merely transcended.
**Caveat:** The Nāth texts affirm one supreme under many names and place all schools beneath it, but they insist that it is reached through the body and the guru, not through scripture or reasoning; a one-truth reading that ignores the body-centred path would misstate them.
