# Where does ignorance (avidyā) reside — in the individual self (jīva) or in Brahman?

`dsp:locus-of-avidya` · `skeleton` · confidence moderate

**Coverage:** A6

## Sides (recorded before any reconciliation)
### [Bhāmatī school (Bhāmatī-prasthāna)](../lineages/bhamati.md)
Ignorance resides in the individual selves, with Brahman as its object; there are as many ignorances as jīvas.
- Pure Brahman, being knowledge itself, cannot be ignorant.
- The jīva and ignorance presuppose each other beginninglessly like seed and sprout, so there is no vicious circle.
- Liberation of one does not liberate all, which many ignorances explain.
**Texts:** 
  - [Bhāmatī](../texts/bhamati.md) — ref: adhyāsa-bhāṣya and passim
  - [Brahmasiddhi](../texts/brahmasiddhi.md) — ref: 1
### [Vivaraṇa school (Vivaraṇa-prasthāna)](../lineages/vivarana.md)
Pure consciousness (Brahman) is both the locus and the object of the one ignorance.
- Only consciousness can be the locus of ignorance; the insentient cannot be ignorant.
- The jīva is itself a product of ignorance and so cannot be its locus without circularity.
- Experience 'I do not know Brahman' shows ignorance veiling consciousness itself.
**Texts:** 
  - [Pañcapādikāvivaraṇa](../texts/pancapadika-vivarana.md) — ref: locus
  - [Saṃkṣepaśārīraka](../texts/samksepa-sariraka.md) — ref: 1.319

## Reconciliation (interpretation layer)
**Status:** partially-reconciled
**Principles:** P1-level, P6-upaya
**Explanation:** The tradition itself treats both as empirical-level explanatory methods (prakriyā) converging on the one self; at the ultimate level there is no ignorance to locate.
Grounded in Sureśvara's principle that any method leading to knowledge of the inner self is valid (BĀU-Vārttika 1.4.402) and Appayya's statement that the ancients, intent on the oneness of the self, were indifferent to how the empirical is explained (SLS opening). GK 2.32 and the Advaita view that avidyā is indescribable make the question one about the empirical explanation.
**The traditions' own objections:** Partisans on each side (Amalānanda for the Bhāmatī; Prakāśātman, Madhusūdana for the Vivaraṇa) argue that the other view is untenable, not merely another method; the reconciliation holds only at the level of the ultimate purport, not of the theories.
