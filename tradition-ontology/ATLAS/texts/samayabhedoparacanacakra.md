The principal Indian doxography of the early schools. It narrates the first schism over the five points of Mahādeva, lists the schools that arose in the second to fourth centuries after the parinirvāṇa, and records their tenets on the Buddha, the arhat, the three times, the intermediate existence, the person and the unconditioned.

## Teachings (9: skeleton 9)

### 1 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

A little over a hundred years after the parinirvāṇa, under King Aśoka at Pāṭaliputra, the Saṅgha split into two over the five points proclaimed by Mahādeva: the Mahāsāṃghika and the Sthavira. In the second century the Mahāsāṃghika divided into the Ekavyavahārika, Lokottaravāda, Kaukkuṭika, Bahuśrutīya, Prajñaptivāda, and (under a second Mahādeva) the Caitika, Aparaśaila and Uttaraśaila; from the third century the Sthaviras divided into the Sarvāstivāda (Hetuvāda) and the Haimavata (the original Sthaviras), then the Vātsīputrīya with its four branches (Dharmottarīya, Bhadrayānīya, Saṃmitīya, Ṣaṇṇagarika), the Mahīśāsaka, the Dharmaguptaka, the Kāśyapīya, and in the fourth century the Sautrāntika (Saṃkrāntika).

### 2 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-2"></a>

The five points of Mahādeva: an arhat may be tempted by others (to an impure emission); he still has non-knowing; he still has doubt; he is brought to understanding by others; and the path arises through an utterance.

### 3 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-3"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The Mahāsāṃghika, Ekavyavahārika, Lokottaravāda and Kaukkuṭika share the root tenets: the Buddhas are supramundane and all Tathāgatas are without outflows; all their words turn the wheel of Dharma; they teach all dharmas with a single sound; their bodies, powers and lifespans are without limit; they never sleep and are always in concentration; bodhisattvas enter the womb by their own will as white elephants and have no thoughts of desire, malice or harm; the nature of mind is originally pure and is defiled by adventitious defilements; there is no intermediate existence; stream-enterers can fall back, arhats cannot; past and future do not exist as entities.

### 4 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-4"></a>

The Sarvāstivāda holds: all that exists is included in name and form; past and future exist as entities; stream-enterers do not fall back, arhats can; there is an intermediate existence; the path of seeing is realized in sixteen moments; not all the Buddha's words are the turning of the wheel; the Buddha's body is not free of outflows in the same sense as his qualities.

### 5 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The Vātsīputrīya holds that the person is neither the same as the aggregates nor apart from them, designated on the basis of the aggregates, sense-bases and elements; dharmas cannot pass from one world to another apart from the person; some formations last for a time, others are momentary.

### 6 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-6"></a>

The (earlier) Mahīśāsaka holds that past and future do not exist, the present and the unconditioned do; there is no intermediate existence; the Buddha is included in the Saṅgha, so a gift to the Saṅgha has great fruit, not a separate gift to the Buddha; there are nine unconditioned dharmas.

### 7 <a id="tea-samayabhedoparacanacakra-7"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence low

The Dharmaguptaka holds that although the Buddha is included in the Saṅgha, a separate gift to the Buddha has greater fruit than one to the Saṅgha; offerings to stūpas bring great fruit; the liberation of Buddhas and disciples is one, though their paths differ; outsiders cannot obtain the five superknowledges.
