Amṛtacandra's 226-verse manual of lay conduct founded on the two standpoints: hiṃsā is the arising of passion in the soul, non-violence its non-arising; the other faults are forms of hiṃsā; sallekhanā undertaken to thin the passions is not self-killing.

### 5 <a id="tea-purusarthasiddhyupaya-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The ultimate standpoint (niścaya) is described as the real (bhūtārtha), the conventional (vyavahāra) as the unreal (abhūtārtha).

### 42 <a id="tea-purusarthasiddhyupaya-42"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence low

Because they injure the soul's own states, falsehood and the rest are all violence; they are named separately only to instruct the disciple.

### 44 <a id="tea-purusarthasiddhyupaya-44"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The non-arising of attachment and the other [passions] is non-violence; their arising is violence — this is the essence of the Jina's teaching.

> aprādurbhāvaḥ khalu rāgādīnāṃ bhavaty ahiṃseti / teṣām evotpattir hiṃseti jināgamasya saṃkṣepaḥ

### 177-178 <a id="tea-purusarthasiddhyupaya-177-178"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence low · _restricted: summary only_

Since death is certain, one who, free of attachment and the like, acts only to thin the passions and the body does not kill himself; but one who, overcome by passion, ends his life by water, fire, poison or weapon, truly commits self-killing.
