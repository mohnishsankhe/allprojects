The sixth Aṅga, teaching by parables and stories: Prince Megha's past life as an elephant, the two tortoises, the four daughters-in-law and the rice grains, the princess Mallī who became the nineteenth Tīrthaṅkara (a woman, in Śvetāmbara belief), Draupadī's story, and others; the second book tells of goddesses who worshipped Mahāvīra.

## Teachings (5: skeleton 5)

### 1.1 <a id="tea-jnatadharmakatha-1-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Prince Megha, newly a monk, lay awake jostled by monks passing his bed and resolved to return home; Mahāvīra reminded him that in a past life, as an elephant, he held up one foot for days during a forest fire so as not to crush a hare that had taken shelter beneath it, and by that compassion gained human birth. Megha was steadied and became a great ascetic.

### 1.4 <a id="tea-jnatadharmakatha-1-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

Two tortoises foraging near a pond are spied by two jackals; the one that put out its limbs was killed, the one that kept its limbs withdrawn in its shell survived. So the monk who guards his senses is safe; the one who lets them out perishes.

### 1.7 <a id="tea-jnatadharmakatha-1-7"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

A merchant gives five rice grains each to his four daughters-in-law and later asks for them back: one threw them away, one ate them, one kept them safe, and one had them sown and returned cartloads. So monks who receive the five great vows may lose, consume, guard or increase them.

### 1.8 <a id="tea-jnatadharmakatha-1-8"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Mallī, daughter of King Kumbha of Mithilā, was the nineteenth Tīrthaṅkara. Courted by six kings, her companions in a former life, she had a hollow golden image of herself made and daily put a morsel of food inside; when the kings came she opened it and the stench overwhelmed them; she taught them that the body is such, and all renounced. She had been born a woman because in her past life as King Mahābala she practised more fasting than her companions by deceit.
