Bhāviveka's 'Heart of the Middle Way' in eleven chapters: after bodhicitta, the muni's vow and the search for the knowledge of reality, it examines and answers the doctrines of the Śrāvakas, Yogācāras, Sāṃkhyas, Vaiśeṣikas, Vedāntins and Mīmāṃsakas, and establishes omniscience — the first Buddhist survey of the Indian schools, preserving an early account of Vedānta.

Chapter on the Yogācāra: the Yogācāras' claim that only consciousness exists and that the other-dependent nature is real is examined and rejected; consciousness too lacks own-nature ultimately, while external things are admitted conventionally.

Chapter on the Sāṃkhya: the Sāṃkhyas' primal matter and the pre-existence of the effect in the cause are examined; an effect already existing needs no production, and an eternal, unconscious pradhāna cannot be the cause of the world.

### 8 <a id="tea-madhyamakahrdaya-8"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence low

(The Vedānta view as Bhāviveka reports it:) the supreme person (puruṣa), the self, is one, all-pervading, eternal and the lord; beings arise from it and dissolve into it; it is known by yogins who see it in themselves, and knowing it one attains immortality.

### 8/2 <a id="tea-madhyamakahrdaya-8-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence low

(Reply:) such a permanent and single self could neither act, change nor be known, and cannot be the cause of the many; whatever in the Vedāntins' teaching is acceptable — non-duality, freedom from conceptual construction — is acceptable only as the emptiness taught by the Buddha.
