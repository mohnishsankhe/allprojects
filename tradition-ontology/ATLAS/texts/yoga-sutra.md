# Yoga Sūtra of Patañjali

`src:yoga-sutra` · `skeleton` · confidence high

**Alternate titles:** Pātañjala Yogasūtra, Yogānuśāsana, Pātañjala Yogaśāstra (with the bhāṣya)
**Original title:** योगसूत्र
**Language:** Sanskrit
**Family:** vedic
**Lineages:** [Pātañjala Yoga (the Yoga darśana)](../lineages/patanjala-yoga.md)
**Genre:** sūtra
**Authors:** 
  - [Patañjali](../teachers/patanjali.md) — role: author; attribution: traditional
**Attribution:** tradition: Composed by Patañjali as a re-teaching (anuśāsana, 1.1) of the yoga first taught by Hiraṇyagarbha (Vijñānabhikṣu, Rāmānanda Sarasvatī, Nāgeśa on 1.1); Patañjali is regarded as an incarnation of Śeṣa (Ananta), the lord of serpents, and identified with the grammarian of the Mahābhāṣya.; scholarly: Most scholars doubt that the Yoga author is the grammarian (c. 2nd c. BCE). Philipp A. Maas argues, following earlier arguments by J. Bronkhorst, that sūtras and bhāṣya form one work — the Pātañjala Yogaśāstra, as the colophons ('pātañjale sāṃkhyapravacane yogaśāstre') call it — by one author, c. 325–425 CE; others treat the sūtras as older than the bhāṣya.; confidence: moderate
**Dates:** Tradition's account: not dated by the tradition (Patañjali as an incarnation of Śeṣa); Scholarly account: c. 325–425 CE together with the bhāṣya (Maas); earlier proposals range from the 2nd c. BCE to the 5th c. CE; (confidence moderate)
**Structure:** 4 pādas: Samādhi (51 sūtras), Sādhana (55), Vibhūti (55), Kaivalya (34) = 195 sūtras in the Vyāsa-based vulgate (Ānandāśrama ed., and every local commentary e-text). Some editions count 196 by treating 'etena śabdādy antardhānam uktam' — part of the bhāṣya on 3.21 in the Ānandāśrama ed. — as a separate sūtra 3.22, shifting the later numbers of pāda 3 by one. This unit uses the 195-sūtra numbering.
**Availability:** digitized-original

The root text of the Yoga darśana. It defines yoga as the stilling of the activities of the mind (1.2); teaches practice and dispassion, devotion to Īśvara and Oṃ, the obstacles and their remedies, the samāpattis and the kinds of samādhi (pāda 1); kriyā-yoga, the five afflictions, karma, the seer and the seen, and the first five of the eight limbs (pāda 2); concentration, meditation, samādhi, saṃyama, the transformations of mind and the powers with their warnings (pāda 3); and the sources of powers, karma and latent tendencies, the reality of objects, the non-self-luminosity of mind, the cloud of dharma and kaivalya (pāda 4).

### ch1 <a id="tea-yoga-sutra-ch1"></a>
`text-verified` · confidence high

Samādhi-pāda (the chapter on samādhi), 1.1–1.51. The chapter opens by announcing the teaching of yoga (1.1) and defining it as the stilling (nirodha) of the mind's activities (vṛtti) (1.2), with the seer then abiding in its own form (1.3–1.4). It sets out the five activities, afflicted or unafflicted (1.5–1.11), and the means of stilling them, practice (abhyāsa) and dispassion (vairāgya), lower and higher (1.12–1.16). It then defines the two samādhis, with full awareness (saṃprajñāta) and beyond it (asaṃprajñāta) (1.17–1.18), gives the birth-based and means-based forms and the five means (1.19–1.22), and teaches devotion to Īśvara, his definition, and the praṇava (1.23–1.29). Next come the nine obstacles (antarāya) and their five companions (1.30–1.31), the single-principle practice against them (1.32), the four attitudes (1.33) and further supports for steadiness (1.34–1.39), followed by coalescence (samāpatti), its four kinds and the samādhi with seed (1.40–1.46), the truth-bearing insight (1.47–1.49), and the seedless samādhi in which even the impression of insight is stilled (1.50–1.51). Its purpose, as the chapter presents it, is to teach yoga to the mind that is already concentrated (samāhita-citta), as Vyāsa says at the start of the second chapter.

### ch2 <a id="tea-yoga-sutra-ch2"></a>
`text-verified` · confidence high

Sādhana-pāda (the chapter on the means), 2.1–2.55. The chapter turns to the practitioner whose mind is not yet concentrated (vyutthita-citta), teaching kriyā-yoga (austerity, self-study, devotion to Īśvara) (2.1–2.2). It then names the five afflictions (kleśa) (2.3), their four states (dormant, attenuated, interrupted, fully active) and the field of ignorance (2.4), each affliction in turn (2.5–2.9), and how they are to be abandoned (2.10–2.11). It gives the karma-store rooted in the afflictions, its ripening in birth, life-span and experience, and the fruit of joy or anguish (2.12–2.14), teaches that all is suffering for the discerning and that future suffering is to be avoided (2.15–2.16), and sets out the seer and the seen, their conjunction, its cause in ignorance and its ending in isolation (kaivalya) (2.17–2.25). Discriminative discernment is the means and its final insight is sevenfold (2.26–2.27). The second half teaches the eight limbs (2.28–2.29): the restraints (yama) with their great vow and the cultivation of the opposite against harmful thoughts (2.30–2.39), the observances (niyama) with their signs (2.32, 2.40–2.45), posture (2.46–2.48), breath-regulation (2.49–2.53) and sense-withdrawal (2.54–2.55), leaving concentration, meditation and samādhi for the next chapter.

### thesis <a id="tea-yoga-sutra-thesis"></a>
`text-verified` · confidence moderate

Thesis of the Yoga Sūtra (with Vyāsa's bhāṣya), decided by the six marks of purport. The text teaches yoga, defined as the restraint (nirodha) of the modifications of the mind (1.2), whose result is the seer's abiding in its own form (1.3); the eight limbs (2.29) and saṃyama (3.4) are its means, discriminative vision (2.26, 3.49, 4.29) its ripening, and kaivalya (2.25, 3.50, 3.55, 4.34) its end. (1) Opening and closing (upakrama-upasaṃhāra): the text opens 'now the teaching of yoga' (1.1), defines yoga and states the seer's abiding in its own form (1.2-1.3); it closes with kaivalya as the establishment in its own form of the power of consciousness (4.34), the same abiding in its own form (svarūpa-pratiṣṭhā) as 1.3 names. (2) Repetition (abhyāsa): kaivalya is stated at 2.25, 3.50, 3.55, 4.26, 4.34; the destruction of afflictions as burnt seeds at 2.10, 3.50, 4.28; discriminative vision at 2.26, 3.49, 3.55, 4.29; the distinction between seer and seen or mind and puruṣa at 2.17-2.23, 3.35, 4.18-4.24; saṃskāra and vāsanā at 1.18, 1.50, 3.9-3.10, 4.8-4.11, 4.27-4.28. (3) Novelty (apūrvatā): no verse marks anything as novel or as knowable by no other means; the only self-designation of this kind is the bhāṣya on 3.4 calling 'saṃyama' the treatise's technical term (paribhāṣā). (4) Stated result (phala): the seer's abiding in its own form (1.3), the light of knowledge up to discriminative vision from the limbs (2.28), results attached to each restraint and observance (2.35-2.45), the results of saṃyama (3.5 and 3.16-3.49), cessation of afflictions and karma and the end of the guṇas' sequence (4.30-4.32), kaivalya (4.34). (5) Praise (arthavāda): no verse is a passage of praise as such; the long list of powers (3.16-3.49) is stated as results, and the text itself places powers below kaivalya: the perceptions of 3.36 are obstacles in samādhi (3.37), kaivalya comes from dispassion even toward overlordship and omniscience (3.50), and invitations from those of high stations are to be met without attachment or pride (3.51). Whether any passage functions as praise is left to the interpretation layer (P7). (6) Reasoned demonstration (upapatti): the account of ignorance as the cause of the conjunction of seer and seen (2.15-2.25); the transformations of the mind and elements (3.9-3.15); the argument for the existence of past and future (4.12), for an object independent of any one mind (4.14-4.16), for the mind's being seen and not self-luminous (4.19-4.21), and for puruṣa from the mind's acting in combination for another (4.24).
