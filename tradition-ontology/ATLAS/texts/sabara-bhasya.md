Śabara's commentary on the whole Mīmāṃsā Sūtra, the root text of all later Mīmāṃsā. On the tarkapāda it defines injunction (codanā) as the statement that impels to action and the sole means of knowing dharma; it preserves the Vṛttikāra's definitions of six means of knowledge, his refutation of idealist and 'empty' views and his proof of the self; it explains apūrva (on MS 2.1.5) and discusses heaven, eligibility and the status of deities.

### 1.1.1 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

When the Veda has been studied, two things present themselves: returning from the teacher's house and inquiring into the Vedic sentences. The sūtra's teaching is: do not return from the teacher's house (yet) — how else would one inquire into the Vedic sentences?

> kiṃ tv adhīte vede dvayam āpatati, gurukulāc ca samāvartitavyaṃ vedavākyāni ca vicārayitavyāni| tatra gurukulān mā samāvartiṣṭa; kathaṃ nu vedavākyāni vicārayed ity evamartho 'yam upadeśaḥ|

### 1.1.2 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Injunction (codanā) is the statement that impels to action. Injunction can make known what is past, present or future, subtle, hidden or remote; no sense faculty can. What is known by it joins a person with the highest good (niḥśreyasa).

> codanā hi bhūtaṃ bhavantaṃ bhaviṣyantaṃ sūkṣmaṃ vyavahitaṃ viprakṛṣṭam ity evañjātīyakam arthaṃ śaknoty avagamayitum, nānyat kiñcanendriyam|

_level: unmarked · standpoint: analytic · path: ritual, general · stage: all · types: sound-language, ultimate_

terms: [codanā](../terms/codana.md), [niḥśreyasa](../terms/nihsreyasa.md) · concepts: [Injunction (codanā / vidhi)](../concepts/vidhi.md), [Dharma (as known through Vedic injunction)](../concepts/dharma.md)

### 1.1.2/2 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-2-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Both a good and a harmful object are made known by injunction: the good is the Jyotiṣṭoma and the like, which lead to the highest good; the harmful is the Śyena, Vajra, Iṣu and the like, which lead to sin, because they are violence and violence is prohibited. The Śyena is not taught as something to be done; it is taught as the means for one who wishes to harm — 'one who practises sorcery should sacrifice with the Śyena' — not 'one should practise sorcery'.

> ubhayam iha codanayā lakṣyate, artho 'narthaś ca| ko 'rthaḥ? yo niḥśreyasāya jyotiṣṭomādiḥ| ko 'narthaḥ? yaḥ pratyavāyāya śyeno vajra iṣur ity evamādiḥ| tatrānartho dharma ukto mā bhūd ity arthagrahaṇam| kathaṃ punar asāv anarthaḥ? hiṃsā hi sā, sā ca pratiṣiddhā| kathaṃ punar asāv anarthaḥ kartavyatayopadiśyate? ucyate - naiva śyenādayaḥ kartavyatayā vijñāyante| yo hi hiṃsitum icchet, tasyāyam abhyupāya iti hi teṣām upadeśaḥ| śyenenābhicaran yajeteti hi samāmananti, nābhicaritavyam iti|

### 1.1.2/3 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-2-3"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

A cognition produced by the Veda is never overturned at another time, for another person, in another state or place; hence it is not false. A human statement about what is beyond the senses has no authority, like the talk of those born blind about colours; teaching (such as Manu's) can arise from delusion, and where there is no delusion it comes from the Veda.

> upadeśo hi vyāmohād api bhavati| asati vyāmohe vedād api bhavati|

### 1.1.5 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Śabara reports that the Vṛttikāra explained the passage otherwise: the means of knowledge are well known and need no examination. Perception is cognition arising when the senses are in contact with the very object cognized; inference is cognition of an unperceived part from seeing another part whose relation is known (of two kinds); śāstra is cognition of an unperceived object from words; comparison produces cognition of the unperceived by similarity; postulation (arthāpatti) is supposing an unseen fact without which a seen or heard fact would not be possible (as Devadatta, alive and not at home, must be outside); absence (abhāva) is the absence of the (other) means of knowledge, for an unperceived object's non-existence.

> arthāpattir api dṛṣṭaḥ śruto vārtho 'nyathā nopapadyata ity arthakalpanā

### 1.1.5/2 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-5-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

(In the Vṛttikāra's passage) against the view that every cognition is objectless like a dream, and that all is empty because object and cognition show no difference of form: the waking cognition of a pillar is well ascertained and not overturned; cognition itself is formless while the external object has form; cognition is inferred, the object is perceived; and a cloth-cognition arises regularly only from threads — so cognition has an object.

### 1.1.5/3 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-5-3"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

(In the Vṛttikāra's passage) the self exists distinct from body and cognition: life-functions are not qualities of the body; desire presupposes earlier experience; one recognizes 'I saw this yesterday', which a series of momentary cognitions could not do. The self is known to itself (svasaṃvedya) and cannot be shown to another, as colour cannot be shown to the blind; the Brāhmaṇa teaches it by 'not this, not this' — negating what another can see (the body).

> svasaṃvedyaḥ sa bhavati| nāsāv anyena śakyate draṣṭum|

### 1.1.5/4 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-5-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Asked 'what is the word in gauḥ?', the revered Upavarṣa answers: the letters g, au and the visarga, since in the world 'word' means what is grasped by the ear. The meaning is understood from the impression left by the letters.

> gakāraukāravisarjanīyā iti bhagavānupavarṣaḥ

### 1.1.5/5 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-5-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

To the objection that 'this sacrificer with his sacrificial implements goes straight to the heavenly world' contradicts perception (the body is burnt here), the answer is that it refers to the one whose body it was — the self, called 'possessor of the implements' through its connection with the body.

### 1.1.25 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-1-1-25"></a>

### 2.1.5 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-2-1-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

By 'injunction' we mean the apūrva. There is an apūrva, since the undertaking is enjoined — 'one desiring heaven should sacrifice'; otherwise the injunction would be pointless, since the sacrifice perishes; if it perished without producing something else, the fruit could not arise without a cause.

> codanety apūrvaṃ brūmaḥ, apūrvaṃ punar asti, yata ārambhaḥ śiṣyate - svargakāmo yajeteti, itarathā hi vidhānam anarthakaṃ syāt, bhaṅgitvād yāgasya

_level: conventional · standpoint: causal · path: ritual, action · stage: all · types: karma-liberation_

### 6.1.1-2 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-6-1-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

In the prima facie discussion, a special region called heaven — without heat or cold, hunger or thirst, reached after death by the meritorious — is said to lack any means of knowledge, Vedic narratives about it being praise joined to injunctions; the conclusion (6.1.2) is that the word 'svarga' denotes happiness (prīti) itself, which it never fails to denote.

### 6.1.5 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-6-1-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

Only humans are eligible for rites: animals do not own property as they please; statements such as 'the gods held a sacrificial session' are arthavāda praising the session, not injunctions for gods or animals.

### 9.1.9 <a id="tea-sabara-bhasya-9-1-9"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The sacrifice, not the deity, is what prompts the rite: that the act gives fruit is known only from the word, which connects the fruit with the act. If the deity prompted it, the deity would have to be embodied and eat; smṛti and usage that speak so rest on mantras and arthavādas not meant that way — 'we have grasped your right hand, Indra' does not establish that Indra has a hand; vocatives serve designation and praise.
