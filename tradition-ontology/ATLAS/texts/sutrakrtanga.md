**Availability:** digitized-original

The second Aṅga, largely polemical: it states and rejects the views of other teachers (materialists, the one-self doctrine, the soul-is-the-body doctrine, non-agency, fatalism, Buddhist momentariness), classifies all rival doctrines under four heads (kriyāvāda, akriyāvāda, ajñānavāda, vinayavāda), warns the monk against temptations, praises Mahāvīra, and in Book II gives the parable of the lotus, Ārdraka's debates with rival ascetics and the Nālandā dialogue with a follower of Pārśva.

## Teachings (33: skeleton 33)

### 1.1.1 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-1-1-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

One should understand and break the bond. What did the hero (Mahāvīra) call the bond, and by knowing what does one break it? One who owns possessions, living or lifeless, or consents to others owning them, or who kills or has others kill, is not freed from suffering.

### 1.1.1/2 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-1-1-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

It states and rejects rival views: that only the five elements exist and the self perishes with them; that there is one self appearing in many forms, as one earth appears as many things; that soul and body are one; that the self is inactive; that the self is a sixth, eternal element; and that there are only momentary aggregates (the Buddhist view). Those who hold them are not free from suffering.

### 1.1.2.14-23 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-1-1-2-14-23"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Some brāhmaṇas and śramaṇas each claim knowledge, yet know nothing of the beings in the world; like a barbarian repeating a non-barbarian's words without their meaning, the ignorant (annāṇiya) do not know the settled meaning; the inquiry of the ignorant cannot be settled in ignorance — unable to instruct themselves, how can they instruct others? ### 1.1.2.24-29 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-1-1-2-24-29"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Another view, the kiriyāvāda of those 'who have left behind concern with karma': sin is incurred only by three means — acting oneself, commissioning another, and approving in mind; what is done unknowingly or without intent is not fully binding; purified by the intention within, one reaches nirvāṇa; even a father who, in need, kills and eats his son is not stained. The Jain text replies that those who sin in mind are not free of thought, and that such teachers are not restrained.

### 2.1 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

In the parable of the lotus, the first man who fails to pluck the lotus holds that the soul is the body (tajjīva-taccharīra): 'upward from the soles of the feet, downward from the tips of the hair, bounded by the skin — this is the soul; when the body dies it does not live'; no one can draw the soul out of the body and show it, like a sword from its sheath or a fibre from a reed; there is no other world, no good or bad deed; the second man holds the five-element view, the third that Īśvara or the self is the cause of all, the fourth that all is fixed by destiny.

### 2.1 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The parable of the lotus (Puṇḍarīka): a great white lotus stands in the middle of a pond; four men from the four directions wade in to pluck it and sink in the mud; a monk standing on the bank calls, 'Rise, O lotus!' and it comes. The pond is the world, the mud desires, the four men the teachers of the soul-is-body view, of the five elements, of a creator-self and of fate; the monk is the one who teaches the true dharma; the lotus is the king (the person to be freed).

### 2.6 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-6"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

Gosāla accuses Mahāvīra of having changed: once he wandered alone, now he preaches to crowds for gain; and he states the Ājīvika rule that an ascetic living alone may use cold water, seeds, food prepared for him and women without sin. Ārdraka answers that Mahāvīra is the same alone or amid crowds, and that those who use these things are householders, not ascetics.

### 2.6 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-6-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Ārdraka, on his way to Mahāvīra, answers in turn Gośāla (who charges Mahāvīra with inconsistency for once living alone and now teaching crowds), Buddhist monks (who say intention alone matters, so that roasting a child mistaken for a gourd would bring no sin), brāhmaṇas (who praise feeding two thousand snātakas), Vedāntin ascetics, and hermits who kill one elephant a year to live without harming many small beings; he refutes each by the standard of non-harm and right knowledge.

### 2.6/2 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-6-2"></a>

Buddhist monks tell Ārdraka that one who roasts a cake of oil-seed thinking it is a man incurs the sin of killing, while one who roasts a child thinking it a gourd does not; Ārdraka rejects this doctrine of intention as unfit for the restrained.

### 2.6/3 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-6-3"></a>

### 2.7 <a id="tea-sutrakrtanga-2-7"></a>
