# Brahmajāla Sutta

**Alternate titles:** The Divine Net

**Location in parent:** DN 1

The 'all-embracing net of views': the ethics for which ordinary people praise the Buddha, then the sixty-two grounds on which ascetics and brahmins theorise about the past and the future (eternalism, partial eternalism, finitude, eel-wriggling, chance origin, the self after death, annihilation, nibbāna here and now), all shown to arise from feeling, contact and craving.

### 1.5-1.6 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-1-5-1-6"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

If others speak against the Buddha, the teaching or the Saṅgha, the monks should not be angry or displeased, nor elated if others praise them; they should explain what is untrue as untrue, or acknowledge what is true as true.

_level: conventional · standpoint: ethical-social · path: general · stage: all · types: ethics_

### 1.7-1.27 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-1-7-1-27"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

When an ordinary person praises the Tathāgata, it is only for trifling matters of mere virtue — abstaining from killing, stealing, unchastity, false and harsh speech, and from the many kinds of low arts, divination and luxury listed in the short, middle and long sections on virtue.

### 1.21-27 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-1-21-27"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Whereas some ascetics and brahmins, living on food given in faith, make their living by wrong livelihood through such low arts (tiracchāna-vijjā) as reading limbs, omens, portents and dreams; marks of gems, weapons, persons and animals; predicting the marches and victories of kings; predicting eclipses of moon, sun and asterisms, their right and wrong courses, meteors, sky-glow, earthquakes and thunder, and their results; forecasting rain, harvest, safety and disease; fixing auspicious days for marriages; and pacificatory and propitiatory rites — the ascetic Gotama refrains from such low arts and wrong livelihood.

### 1.30-1.37 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-1-30-1-37"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Eternalism (sassatavāda) on four grounds: some ascetics and brahmins, by ardent effort and right attention, reach a concentration in which they recall up to a hundred thousand past lives, or ten, or forty aeons of contraction and expansion, and conclude that self and world are eternal, barren, firm as a pillar, while beings transmigrate; the fourth kind reaches the same conclusion by reasoning. The Tathāgata knows where such grounds lead in the next life, knows what is beyond them, and is freed by not grasping.

### 1.30-3.29 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-1-30-3-29"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Ascetics and brahmins who theorise about the past and the future do so on sixty-two grounds: eternalism, partial eternalism, the world as finite or infinite, evasive 'eel-wriggling', chance origination (eighteen about the past); the self after death as percipient, non-percipient or neither, annihilationism, and nibbāna here and now identified with sense-pleasure or the jhānas (forty-four about the future).

### 2.1-2.15 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-2-1-2-15"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Partial eternalism (ekacca-sassatavāda) on four grounds: (1) the being first reborn in an empty Brahmā-palace after the world's contraction thinks 'I am Brahmā, the maker, the creator, the father of beings', and later beings agree; one who falls from there and recalls it holds Brahmā permanent and himself impermanent; (2) gods corrupted by play (khiḍḍā-padosika) and (3) gods corrupted by mind (mano-padosika) fall from their state and judge the others permanent; (4) the reasoner holds eye, ear, nose, tongue and body an impermanent self, but mind or consciousness a permanent self.

### 2.23-2.29 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-2-23-2-29"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Eel-wriggling (amarāvikkhepa) on four grounds: some who do not truly know what is skilful and unskilful refuse to declare anything, (1) from fear and disgust of false speech, (2) from fear and disgust of grasping, since a declaration might arouse desire or aversion, (3) from fear of being cross-examined by clever debaters; (4) one who is dull and stupid, asked about another world, spontaneously born beings, fruit of deeds or the Tathāgata after death, replies 'I do not say it is so, nor thus, nor otherwise, nor not, nor not not'.

### 3.29-3.31 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-3-29-3-31"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

These sixty-two grounds cover all theories about the past and the future: there is none outside them. The Tathāgata understands them, where they lead, and what is beyond them; not misapprehending, he knows peace within, and having understood the arising, passing, gratification, danger and escape of feelings, is freed by not grasping. The truths he makes known are deep, hard to see, peaceful, sublime, beyond the sphere of reasoning (atakkāvacara).

### 3.32-3.71 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-3-32-3-71"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Every one of these views is only the feeling of those who do not know and see, the agitation of those caught by craving; each is conditioned by contact; without contact they could not be experienced; experienced through the six sense-bases, feeling conditions craving, craving grasping, grasping existence, existence birth, and birth ageing, death and suffering.

_level: conventional · standpoint: causal · path: knowledge · stage: all · types: consciousness-mind, dispute_

### 3.44-3.71 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-3-44-3-71"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Those views are only the feeling of those who do not know and see, the agitation and writhing of those sunk in craving; they are conditioned by contact, and none of them could be experienced without contact. Those theorists experience them through the six fields of contact, and with feeling as condition comes craving, clinging, becoming, birth, ageing and death. The Tathāgata knows these grounds, their destiny and what lies beyond them, and is freed without clinging.

### 3.72-3.73 <a id="tea-brahmajala-sutta-3-72-3-73"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

All these theorists are caught in this net as the fish of a pond in a fine-meshed net. The Tathāgata's body stands with the cord that leads to becoming cut; while the body lasts gods and humans see him, at its breaking up they will not — as mangoes follow a stalk that is cut.
