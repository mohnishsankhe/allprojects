Kundakunda's treatise on the pure self (samaya): from the standpoint of ultimate reality (niścaya) the soul is a pure knower, untouched by karma, neither doer nor enjoyer of material karma, while the conventional standpoint (vyavahāra), which ascribes bondage, conduct and stages to it, is needed only to teach. It proceeds through the nine categories (soul and non-soul, doer and deed, merit and demerit, inflow, stopping, shedding, bondage, liberation) to the all-pure knowledge.

### 145-146 <a id="tea-samayasara-145-146"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

Bad karma is 'ill-natured', and good karma, you think, is 'well-natured' — but how can what leads into saṃsāra be well-natured? A fetter of gold binds a man as much as one of iron; so good and bad deeds alike bind the soul.

### kartr-karma <a id="tea-samayasara-kartr-karma"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The chapter on doer and deed: the soul is the doer only of its own states (bhāva); material karma is done by matter; to think 'I am the doer of the other' is ignorance. Karmic matter and the soul's impure states are mutually occasioning (nimitta) without either becoming the other.
