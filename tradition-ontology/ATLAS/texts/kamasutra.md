The śāstra of the third aim. Its second chapter teaches that a man with a span of a hundred years should divide his time and pursue the three aims so that they support and do not harm one another — learning and wealth in youth, love in early manhood, dharma and liberation in old age (1.2.1–4) — that of the three each earlier is weightier (1.2.14), and answers objections of Mīmāṃsakas and of Lokāyatikas, who say dharma's fruit is future and doubtful: 'better a pigeon today than a peacock tomorrow' (1.2.21–24).

### 1.2.1-4 <a id="tea-kamasutra-1-2-1-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

A man's life is a hundred years; dividing his time, he should pursue the three aims so that they are bound together and do not harm one another: learning and the acquisition of wealth in childhood, love in youth, dharma and liberation in old age.

### 1.2.14-15 <a id="tea-kamasutra-1-2-14-15"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

When the three conflict, each earlier one is weightier (dharma over artha, artha over kāma) — but for a king artha comes first, since the world's livelihood rests on it.

### 1.2.21-24 <a id="tea-kamasutra-1-2-21-24"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The Lokāyatikas say: dharma should not be practised, for its fruit lies in the future and is doubtful; who but a fool would give away what is in his hand? Better a pigeon today than a peacock tomorrow; better a sure copper coin than a doubtful gold piece.
