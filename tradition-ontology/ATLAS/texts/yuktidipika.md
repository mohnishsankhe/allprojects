The most extensive old commentary on the SK. It defends Sāṃkhya against rival schools, preserves the divergent views of earlier Sāṃkhya teachers (Vārṣagaṇya, Vindhyavāsin, Pañcādhikaraṇa, Paurika, the Sāṃkhya teacher Patañjali), enumerates the sixty topics of the Ṣaṣṭitantra and gives a list of teachers between Pañcaśikha and Īśvarakṛṣṇa.

## Teachings (10: skeleton 10)

### 22 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-22"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The teachers disagree on emergence: some place an indescribable principle between pradhāna and mahat; for Patañjali, Pañcādhikaraṇa and Vārṣagaṇa mahat arises from pradhāna; for Vindhyavāsin six non-specifics (ahaṃkāra and the five subtle elements) arise from mahat. All derive the organs from ahaṃkāra, but Pañcādhikaraṇa holds them elemental; Vārṣagaṇya holds the subtle elements cumulative; Vindhyavāsin holds the organs all-pervading, the instrument elevenfold, apprehension in manas, and the functions of saṅkalpa, abhimāna and adhyavasāya one.

### 39 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-39"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

On transmigration the teachers disagree: for Pañcādhikaraṇa a conveying (ātivāhika) subtle body, permanent, enters semen and blood at conception and is clothed and unclothed by the perishable outer body; for Patañjali a subtle body leads the organs to the womb and ceases, another arising at death; for Vindhyavāsin, since the organs are all-pervading, birth is their functioning at the seed-place and death its abandonment, so there is no subtle body.

### 43 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-43"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The dispositions are innate, natural or acquired. Innate: the supreme sage's knowledge, arising with his body like light with a lamp; likewise the virtue of Bhṛgu and others and the dispassion of Sanaka and others. Natural: Āsuri's dispassion, arising from contact with the supreme sage; Nandin's power from contact with Maheśvara; Nahuṣa's virtue from contact with Agastya. Acquired: ours. Against Vindhyavāsin, who held even the sage's knowledge to be acquired, and Pañcādhikaraṇa, who derived all from another.

### 56 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-56"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Objection: the Sāṃkhya teacher Paurika holds that each puruṣa has its own pradhāna producing its body, and that when the pradhāna of the great-bodied one operates the others operate, and when it ceases they cease. Reply: one prakṛti is accepted, because there is no means of knowledge for many — they are beyond the senses and there is no unambiguous inferential mark.

### 70 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-70"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The supreme sage gave the teaching not for merit, wealth or pleasure, nor for his own liberation, which his innate knowledge secured, but out of compassion, seeing Āsuri afflicted by the three sufferings and wishing that, as for himself, suffering should cease for Āsuri and through him for others. Because the doctrine began with the Blessed One, its lineage cannot be recounted even in hundreds of thousands of years.

### 71 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-71"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The doctrine came through Hārīta, Vāddhali, Kairāta, Paurika, Ṛṣabheśvara, Pañcādhikaraṇa, Patañjali, Vārṣagaṇya, Kauṇḍinya, Mūka and others; since the older works had grown too long for people of short life, Īśvarakṛṣṇa, out of compassion, gathered the statements of the Ṣaṣṭitantra, separated from their tales, into seventy verses.

### 72 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-72"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Because Nārāyaṇa, Manu, Janaka, Vasiṣṭha, Dvaipāyana and other teachers accepted, taught and praised pradhāna, puruṣa and the other categories, Īśvarakṛṣṇa examined them by reasoning, following the scripture of the supreme sage and giving precedence to the three means of knowledge.

### intro.3-9 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-intro-3-9"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The sage told the great doctrine to the brahmin Āsuri, who sought the truth, for the removal of the three sufferings; since it could not be mastered even in hundreds of years, wise sages condensed it; rival teachers built intricate arguments in their own treatises; therefore Īśvarakṛṣṇa made this concise work called the Seventy, which is nevertheless a complete treatise.

### intro.6-7 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-intro-6-7"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence moderate

The opponents of the doctrine are those who hold puruṣa, Īśvara or atoms (as the cause), the destructionists (vaināśika), the prākṛtikas and those who take a modification to be the self; the teachers built difficult arguments to defeat them.

### intro.10-12 <a id="tea-yuktidipika-intro-10-12"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The ten fundamental topics are: the existence of pradhāna, its oneness, its purposiveness, (puruṣa's) distinctness, being for another, the plurality (of puruṣas), separation, conjunction, the remaining function, and non-agency; with the fivefold error, the ninefold contentment, the twenty-eightfold incapacity of the instruments and the eight accomplishments, these make sixty topics.
