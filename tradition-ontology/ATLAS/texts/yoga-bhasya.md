**Availability:** digitized-original

The oldest and authoritative commentary. It defines yoga as samādhi and names the five levels of mind (1.1); analyzes the vṛttis, the kleśas and their states (with a fifth, 'burnt-seed' state), karma and its fruition; gives the fourfold scheme modelled on medicine (2.15) and the sevenfold final insight (2.27); lists seated postures (2.46); explains saṃyama and the powers, the four kinds of yogin and the invitation of the celestials (3.51), and a detailed cosmology (3.26); and argues against Buddhist views of a momentary mind and of objects as mind-only (1.32, 4.14–24).

## Teachings (120: skeleton 120)

### 1.1 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Yoga is samādhi, and samādhi is a property of the mind at all its levels. The levels of the mind are the scattered (kṣipta), the stupefied (mūḍha), the distracted (vikṣipta), the one-pointed (ekāgra) and the stilled (niruddha). Samādhi in a distracted mind, being subordinate to distraction, does not count as yoga. Samādhi in a one-pointed mind, which illumines a real object, weakens the afflictions, loosens the bonds of karma and turns toward stilling, is called samprajñāta yoga (with reasoning, reflection, bliss or I-am-ness); when all activities are stilled, it is asamprajñāta samādhi.

### 1.2 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The mind, having the nature of illumination, activity and inertia, has three guṇas. Mixed with rajas and tamas it loves power and objects; pervaded by tamas it tends to demerit, ignorance, attachment and powerlessness; with the veil of delusion gone and touched by rajas it tends to merit, knowledge, dispassion and power; freed from the last stain of rajas and established in itself it is only the discernment of the difference between sattva and puruṣa and tends to the meditation called the cloud of dharma — which meditators call the highest reflection (prasaṃkhyāna). The power of consciousness is unchanging, does not pass over [into objects], has objects shown to it, and is pure and infinite; discriminative discernment, being of the nature of sattva, is its opposite. Detached even from that discernment, the mind restrains it too; only impressions then remain: this is seedless samādhi, called asamprajñāta because nothing is cognized in it.

### 1.4 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

In emergence the puruṣa has activities no different from the mind's. The mind, like a magnet, serves the puruṣa, its owner, by mere proximity, by being seen; their beginningless relation is the cause of the puruṣa's awareness of the mind's activities.

### 1.5 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-5"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Afflicted activities are caused by the afflictions and become the field in which karmic residue accumulates; unafflicted ones have discernment as their object and oppose the operation of the guṇas. Unafflicted activities occur even in a stream of afflicted ones, and afflicted ones in the gaps of unafflicted ones. Activities make impressions and impressions make activities: so the wheel of activity and impression turns ceaselessly, until the mind, its task complete, abides as the self does, or dissolves.

### 1.7 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-7"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Perception is the mind's activity, coloured by an external thing through the channel of the senses, that chiefly ascertains the particular in an object having general and particular aspects; its result is the puruṣa's awareness of the mind's activity. Inference chiefly ascertains the general, by a relation present in like cases and absent in unlike ones (the moon and stars move, because they change place, like Caitra). Testimony is the activity arising in a hearer from the words of a reliable speaker who has seen or inferred the matter; if the original speaker had not seen or inferred it, the testimony wavers.

### 1.8 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-8"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Error is not valid cognition because valid cognition sublates it, as seeing one moon sublates seeing two. This is five-jointed ignorance — ignorance, I-am-ness, attachment, aversion and clinging — which under their own names are darkness (tamas), delusion (moha), great delusion (mahāmoha), gloom (tāmisra) and blinding gloom (andhatāmisra).

### 1.10 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-10"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Sleep is a particular cognition, because on waking it is recollected: 'I slept happily; my mind is clear'; 'I slept badly; my mind is dull and wanders'; 'I slept heavily, stupefied; my limbs are heavy'. Without an experience of cognition there could be no such recollection. In samādhi sleep too must be stilled, like other cognitions.

### 1.11 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-11"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Memory arises from an impression begun by a cognition that shows both the grasped and the grasping; the buddhi is chiefly the form of the grasping, memory chiefly that of the grasped. Memory is twofold: in dream the thing remembered is imagined; in waking it is not. All memories arise from experiences of the five activities, and all activities have the nature of pleasure, pain and delusion — attachment follows on pleasure, aversion on pain, and delusion is ignorance. All these activities are to be stilled; when they are stilled there is samprajñāta or asamprajñāta samādhi.

### 1.12 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-12"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The river of the mind flows both ways: it flows toward good and flows toward evil. Borne toward kaivalya and sloping toward discrimination, it flows toward good; borne toward saṃsāra and sloping toward non-discrimination, toward evil. Dispassion dams the stream toward objects; practice in seeing discrimination opens the stream of discrimination. So stilling depends on both.

### 1.15 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-15"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Lower dispassion is the consciousness of mastery — without engagement, with nothing to reject or take up — of a mind that, by the strength of reflection, sees the faults of objects, whether seen (women, food and drink, power) or heard of in scripture (heaven, the bodiless state, absorption in prakṛti), even when in contact with divine or earthly objects.

### 1.16 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-16"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Higher dispassion belongs to one who, seeing the faults of seen and scriptural objects, and with understanding nourished by the practice of seeing the puruṣa, is detached from the guṇas themselves, manifest and unmanifest. It is only the clarity of knowledge; when it arises the yogin thinks: 'What was to be attained is attained; the afflictions to be destroyed are destroyed; the close-jointed chain of becoming — by whose continuance one is born and dies, dies and is born — is cut.' Dispassion is the utmost limit of knowledge, and kaivalya is inseparable from it.

### 1.17 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-17"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Reasoning (vitarka) is the mind's gross engagement with its support; reflection (vicāra) the subtle; bliss (ānanda) is joy; I-am-ness (asmitā) is awareness of a single self. The first samādhi, with reasoning, is accompanied by all four; the second, lacking reasoning, is with reflection; the third, lacking reflection, is with bliss; the fourth, lacking that, is I-am-ness alone. All these samādhis have a support.

### 1.18 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-18"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

When all activities have set, the stilling of the mind with only impressions remaining is asamprajñāta samādhi; its means is higher dispassion. Practice with a support cannot achieve it, so the cognition of cessation, which has no object, is made the support; preceded by its practice the mind becomes without support, as if non-existent: this is seedless samādhi.

### 1.19 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-19"></a>

### 1.20 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-20"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

For yogins [samādhi] comes from means. Faith is the serenity of the mind; like a kind mother it protects the yogin. In one who has faith and seeks discrimination, energy arises; in one with energy, mindfulness is present; with mindfulness present the mind, undisturbed, becomes concentrated; to the concentrated mind comes discerning insight, by which one knows things as they are. From practising that, and from dispassion toward its objects, asamprajñāta samādhi arises. These yogins are of nine kinds: mild, middling or intense in means, each with mild, middling or intense ardor.

### 1.24 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-24"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Afflictions, good and bad actions, their fruit and the residues exist in the mind but are ascribed to the puruṣa, as victory or defeat of soldiers is ascribed to their lord. Īśvara is the special puruṣa untouched by such experience. He is not like the liberated, who had an earlier state of bondage, nor like those merged in prakṛti, who may be bound later: he is always free and always the Lord. His eternal pre-eminence rests on his taking up of excellent sattva; scripture is its evidence, and scripture rests on that sattva, a beginningless relation. His lordship has no equal and no superior: two equal lords wishing opposite things at once could not both succeed; so he in whom lordship reaches its limit is Īśvara.

### 1.25 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-25"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The seed of omniscience — the supersensible grasp, lesser or greater, of past, future and present, singly or together — grows; where it is unsurpassed is the omniscient one, for what admits of degrees has a limit, like magnitude. Inference establishes only the general; his name and particulars must be sought from scripture. Though he has no purpose of his own, his purpose is compassion for beings: 'At the dissolutions I will lift up transmigrating beings by teaching knowledge and dharma.' So it is said: 'The first knower, the blessed supreme sage, out of compassion assumed a created mind and taught the doctrine to Āsuri, who wished to know.'

### 1.27 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-1-27"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Īśvara is what the praṇava designates. The relation of designator and designated is not made by convention but stands fixed, like that of a lamp and its light; convention only shows the relation as it stands, as the relation of father and son is shown by 'this is his father, this his son'. In other creations too the convention is made in dependence on the same capacity; the upholders of scripture hold that the relation of word and meaning is eternal because usage is eternal.

### 2.15 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-2-15"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Every pleasure is pervaded by attachment and dependent on means, so there is karmic residue from attachment, and likewise from aversion and delusion; and 'enjoyment is impossible without harming beings'. Dispassion cannot be made by indulgence, for attachments and the senses' skill grow with it — like one afraid of a scorpion's sting who is bitten by a snake. So there are the sufferings of change, of anguish and of impressions, a beginningless stream that afflicts only the yogin, for the wise one is like an eyeball: a wool thread that hurts the eye does not hurt other limbs. Seeing himself and all beings carried along by it, the yogin takes refuge in right seeing. As medical science has four parts — disease, its cause, health and remedy — so this śāstra has four: saṃsāra, its cause, liberation and the means of liberation. Saṃsāra full of suffering is what is to be avoided; the conjunction of pradhāna and puruṣa is its cause; the final cessation of the conjunction is avoidance; right seeing is the means. Karma is of four kinds: black, white-and-black, white, and neither white nor black. Black belongs to the wicked. White-and-black is accomplished by external means, where accumulation of residue comes through harming and helping others. White belongs to those given to austerity, self-study and meditation: it depends on the mind alone, not on external means, and arises without harming others. ### 4.10 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-4-10"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The latent tendencies are beginningless, because the self-wish 'may I not cease, may I be', seen in all, is not natural: how could a creature just born, which has not experienced death, fear death except through memory of past pain? Some hold that the mind, like a lamp in a jar or a palace, contracts and expands to the size of the body, which makes the intermediate state and transmigration intelligible; the teacher (ācārya) holds that only the activity of the all-pervading mind contracts and expands. Its causes are external (praise, gifts, salutation, dependent on body and means) and internal (faith and the like, dependent on the mind alone); as it is said: 'the friendliness and the rest, which are the abodes (vihāra) of meditators, independent of external means, bring about the highest dharma'. Of the two the mental is stronger: who could empty the Daṇḍaka forest by bodily action, or drink the ocean like Agastya, without the strength of mind?

### 4.11 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-4-11"></a>

From merit comes pleasure, from demerit pain; from pleasure attachment, from pain aversion; thence effort, by which one, stirring in mind, speech or body, helps or harms others; thence again merit and demerit, pleasure and pain, attachment and aversion: thus turns the six-spoked wheel of saṃsāra, whose driver is ignorance, the root of all afflictions. ### 4.21 <a id="tea-yoga-bhasya-4-21"></a>

If one mind were grasped by another, by what would the cognition of cognition be grasped? — an infinite regress, and confusion of memories. So the destructionists (vaināśika), denying the puruṣa who is aware of the buddhi, throw everything into confusion. Some of them imagine a mere being that lays down these five aggregates (skandha) and takes up others, and then shrink from it; and saying 'I will live the holy life under a teacher for great disenchantment with the aggregates, for dispassion, for non-arising, for peace', they deny the very being who would do so. The Sāṃkhya, Yoga and other doctrines accept, in their own words, a puruṣa, the owner and enjoyer of the mind.

The mind, coloured by the object and, being itself an object, joined to the puruṣa by its own activity, appears as both object and subject, conscious and non-conscious, like a crystal. Permanence is twofold: the unchanging permanence of the puruṣa (kūṭastha-nityatā) and the changing permanence of the guṇas (pariṇāmi-nityatā). Is there an end to the sequence of saṃsāra?
