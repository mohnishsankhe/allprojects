Vācaspati Miśra's gloss on the Nyāyavārttika. Its opening verses invoke Śiva (Pinākin) and Akṣapāda and state the aim of rescuing Uddyotakara's aged words sunk in the mire of bad commentaries; it answers Dharmakīrti's school, reads NS 1.1.4 as covering indeterminate and determinate perception, and names the twenty-one kinds of pain whose absolute cessation is the goal.

## Teachings (3: skeleton 3)

### 1.1.4 <a id="tea-nyaya-varttika-tatparya-tika-1-1-4"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

The word 'vyavasāyātmaka' in the definition of perception directly denotes determinate (savikalpaka) perception (and 'avyapadeśya' the indeterminate); Vācaspati says he explains this following the path shown by his teacher Trilocana, though Bhāṣya and Vārttika did not spell it out.

> vyavasāyātmakapadaṃ sākṣāt savikalpakasya vācakam

### 1.1.1 (on 'śama') <a id="tea-nyaya-varttika-tatparya-tika-1-1-1-2"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

'Peace' (śama) here is the absolute cessation of pain, which is divided into twenty-one kinds; the supremely compassionate sage composed the śāstra wishing to lift out the world sunk in the mire of pain.

> atra caikaviṃśatiprabhedabhinnasya duḥkhasyātyantikī nivṛttiḥ śamaḥ

### 1.1.1 (opening) <a id="tea-nyaya-varttika-tatparya-tika-1-1-1"></a>
`skeleton` · confidence high

Vācaspati salutes Śiva — all-pervading, all-powerful, bearer of the bow Pināka, maker, knower and destroyer of the universe — and Akṣapāda, rich in dharma, knowledge, dispassion and lordship; he wishes to earn some merit by rescuing the very old cows of Uddyotakara (his words), sunk in the mire of hard-to-cross bad commentaries. He explains that Uddyotakara wrote because the śāstra, though explained by Pakṣilasvāmin, had been obscured by later bad reasoners such as Dignāga.

> icchāmi kim api puṇyaṃ dustarakunibandhapaṅkamagnānām / uddyotakaragavīnām atijaratīnāṃ samuddharaṇāt
