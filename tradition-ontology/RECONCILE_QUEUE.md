## RQ-U50-05 — Is Advaita Vedānta — the doctrine of māyā — 'Buddhism in disguise' (pracchanna-bauddha)?  
`dsp:advaita-crypto-buddhism` · skeleton

- **lin:bhedabheda**: Bhāskara: the doctrine of māyā — disputed, cut off from its root and 'sung by the Mahāyāna Buddhists' — leads people astray.
- **lin:dvaita**: Madhva and Jayatīrtha: the māyā-teacher and the Mādhyamika are both to be ignored — what is the void for the voidist is Brahman for the māyā-teacher; both ultimates are attributeless and defined only by exclusion; the voidists' saṃvṛti is the māyā-teachers' māyā, alike beginningless, indeterminable, concealing and projecting. Liberation cannot come from knowing the void.
- **lin:puranic**: The Padma Purāṇa (Uttarakhaṇḍa), in Śiva's list of heretical teachings: 'māyāvāda is a false teaching, called Buddhism in disguise; I taught it in the Kali age in the form of a brāhmaṇa'.
- **lin:gaudiya-vaisnava**: Jīva Gosvāmī: the doctrine that the world is false and the Lord's lordship imagined by ignorance is the view the Gītā (16.8) calls godless; it conflicts with the Lord's word and is rightly called māyāvāda, which the Padma Purāṇa calls Buddhism in disguise. Caitanya (as the Gauḍīya texts present him) rejects Śaṅkara's commentary as hiding the sūtras' direct sense.
- **lin:visistadvaita**: Vedānta Deśika opens the Śatadūṣaṇī urging followers of Rāmānuja's path to 'strive on every side for victory over the crypto-Buddhists (pracchanna-bauddha)'.
- **lin:samkhya**: Vijñānabhikṣu: the māyā doctrine of the 'modern' Vedāntins is not the Brahmasūtra's teaching; with the Padma Purāṇa he counts it among heterodox (crypto-Buddhist) teachings.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Advaita is not Buddhism: Śaṅkara refutes all three Buddhist doctrines — the realist, the idealist and the void — and dismisses the void as opposed by every means of knowledge; against the idealist he upholds external objects and the undeniable, self-established witness; memory proves one enduring knower. The non-dual reality free of knower, known and knowing 'was not spoken by the Buddha': the Buddhist denial of external objects is only near to non-duality, which is to be known from the Upaniṣads alone. Brahman is being (sat) and consciousness, the self that no one can deny — not a void and not a momentary cognition.
- Candidate readings:
  - P8-six-marks: read by opening and close, repetition, novelty, fruit, praise and reasoning, the purport of Śaṅkara's commentaries is the Upaniṣadic Brahman as existent (sat), which the Buddhist texts deny — Advaita's own claim; the critics apply the same marks to find māyā/void as the purport.
  - P1-level: both systems use a two-truths frame; the charge concerns what is placed at the ultimate — the critics treat 'attributeless' as equivalent to 'void', Advaita and the Buddhists both reject that equivalence.
  - P6-upaya (the Padma Purāṇa's own framing): māyāvāda as a teaching given at the Lord's command to delude the asuras — a Purāṇic account the Advaitins do not accept as describing their teaching.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the critics hold that Advaita's attributeless Brahman and māyā equal the Buddhists' void and saṃvṛti; Advaita holds that Brahman as being-consciousness and the undeniable witness are exactly what the Buddhists deny.

## RQ-U16-1 — Is the difference between Brahman and the soul due to adjuncts, natural, or inconceivable?  
`dsp:aupadhika-vs-svabhavika-bhedabheda` · skeleton

- **lin:bhedabheda**: Bhāskara: non-difference is natural, difference is due to real adjuncts (aupādhika); when the adjuncts fall away at death the soul is one with Brahman.
- **lin:bhedabheda**: Yādavaprakāśa: both are natural (svābhāvika); Brahman itself becomes Īśvara, souls and matter (as reported by Rāmānuja).

- **lin:visistadvaita**: Rāmānuja: both forms are faulty — Bhāskara's makes Brahman itself suffer through its adjuncts, Yādava's makes Brahman itself undergo change and imperfection; the relation is that of soul and body (inseparable attribute).

- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: part–whole (difference) vs substance (non-difference) as complementary standpoints — rejected by Bhāskara, who makes difference conditional, and by Rāmānuja, who denies non-difference in substance
  - P1-level: difference at the conventional level, non-difference at the ultimate — this is Advaita's reading and all these schools reject it as making difference unreal
  - Gauḍīya 'acintya' as a meta-position: the tension is itself affirmed as beyond reason
- Note: not yet reconciled: each school's account of the cause of difference is a claim about what is real, and each rejects the others'.

## RQ-035 — Does liberation contain bliss (and consciousness)?  
`dsp:bliss-in-liberation` · skeleton

- **lin:nyaya**: Mainstream Nyāya: liberation is absolute release from pain; there is no proof of an eternal pleasure manifested in it, and one who strives for liberation out of attachment to pleasure is not freed; the liberated self has neither pleasure nor cognition. Pleasure bound up with pain is to be relinquished like food mixed with honey and poison.
- **lin:vaisesika**: Liberation is the absence of contact with a body and of new appearance when adṛṣṭa is exhausted; a quiescence like fire whose fuel is burnt (no special qualities of the self remain).
- **lin:nyaya**: Bhāsarvajña (a minority within Nyāya): liberation is the absolute cessation of pain qualified by the experience of eternal pleasure.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Liberation is the realization of the self as Brahman, being-consciousness-bliss; a liberation without consciousness or bliss would be no better than a stone's state — a charge pressed against Nyāya (e.g. in Śrīharṣa's Naiṣadhīyacarita, as usually cited).
- **lin:bhatta-mimamsa**: Pārthasārathi: no — release has no cognition and no bliss; it is like dreamless sleep without return, a human goal because pain ceases; Kumārila: release is not an enjoyment of pleasure, which would be a perishable heaven.
- **lin:bhatta-mimamsa**: Some Bhāṭṭas (reported in the Śāstradīpikā): yes — the self's innate bliss, overpowered in saṃsāra, becomes manifest and is enjoyed through the mind in release; Kumārila's verse states another's view.
- **lin:prabhakara-mimamsa**: No — scripture does not make known accomplished things, and no supreme bliss is experienced by the embodied; release is valuable as the end of sorrow.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Yes — liberation is the realization of Brahman, whose nature is bliss.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint / terminological: Nyāya denies pleasure as an adventitious quality produced by contact with objects; Advaita affirms bliss as the self's nature, not a produced quality — the two may partly talk past each other.
  - P1-level: Advaita could read Nyāya's account as describing liberation negatively from the empirical standpoint.
  - The Śāstradīpikā itself notes that on either view release is a human goal and differs from saṃsāra by the absence of pain.
  - P1-level: Advaita's bliss belongs to the absolute self, not to an experience of a knower — a reading the Mīmāṃsakas reject.
- Note: Not yet reconciled.

## RQ-071 — Is sound (the word) eternal?  
`dsp:eternality-of-sound` · skeleton

- **lin:nyaya**: No: sound has a beginning, is sense-perceived and is spoken of like produced things; it is a non-eternal quality of ākāśa. The word–meaning relation is conventional (later: the Lord's convention).
- **lin:vaisesika**: No: sound is produced from conjunction, disjunction and sound; understanding meaning from words is conventional.
- **lin:mimamsa**: Yes: the word (and its phonemes) is eternal and its relation to meaning original; what is produced is only its manifestation by utterance.
- **lin:vyakarana**: The meaning-bearing word (sphoṭa) is eternal, manifested by sounds (Bhartṛhari).
- **lin:mimamsa**: Words — the letters — are eternal and only manifested by utterance: utterance serves to convey to another; the same word is recognized everywhere; utterances are counted as repetitions of one word; no cause of destruction is known; the Veda speaks of eternal speech.
- **lin:nyaya**: Sound is a non-eternal quality of ether: it has a beginning, is perceived by a sense and is spoken of as produced; the Veda's authority comes from its reliable author, not from eternal words.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: Everything produced is momentary; words signify by convention; no eternal, authorless word exists.
- **lin:vyakarana**: The word is eternal, but as the sphoṭa manifested by sounds, not as the audible letters.
- **lin:vyakarana**: Word, meaning and their relation are eternal (Kātyāyana's first vārttika); the eternal word is the sphoṭa, while the audible sounds that manifest it arise and perish.
- **lin:mimamsa**: The letters themselves are eternal and only manifested by utterance.
- **lin:nyaya**: Sound is a non-eternal quality of ether: it has a beginning and is spoken of as produced.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: distinguish produced sound (dhvani) from the word as a type; Mīmāṃsakas grant that manifesting sounds are produced, and Naiyāyikas that the universal of a phoneme is eternal.
  - P2-standpoint: grammarians and Mīmāṃsakas agree on eternality and differ on its bearer (sphoṭa vs letters); Nyāya's 'sound' may be the audible noise (dhvani) that Mīmāṃsā too treats as produced (MS 1.1.17) — a partial overlap each side would qualify.
  - P1-level: what is eternal (the word as meaning-bearer) and what is produced (the audible sound) are distinguished by the grammarians; Nyāya speaks of the audible sound only.
- Note: Not yet reconciled.

## RQ-U38-02 — Do past and future dharmas exist?  
`dsp:existence-in-three-times` · skeleton

- **lin:sarvastivada**: Yes: all dharmas exist in all three times as real entities; the times are distinguished by activity (kāritra).
- **lin:sautrantika**: No: only the present exists; the conditioned 'arises having not been, and having been ceases'; results arise from the transformed series preceded by the karma.
- **lin:kasyapiya**: Partly: past karma that has not yet given its fruit exists; the rest of the past and the future do not (the Vibhajyavāda position).
- **lin:theravada**: No: 'everything exists' is refuted.
- **lin:mahasanghika**: No: past and future have no real existence (Vasumitra).
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: the Vaibhāṣika speaks of a dharma's own-nature (substance, dravyārthika) which does not change, the Sautrāntika of its activity and moment (mode, paryāyārthika); the Vaibhāṣika itself distinguishes the times only by activity — rejected by both, since each affirms or denies real existence, not a standpoint.

- **lin:yogacara**: Yes: without a continuous, neutral consciousness holding the seeds, one could not explain the appropriation of a new body, the survival of seeds through the mindless attainments, the simultaneous occurrence of several cognitions, the karmic process and death.

## RQ-077 — Are there objects external to cognition?

## RQ-102 — Does scripture (śruti) teach the Sāṃkhya's unconscious pradhāna as the cause of the world?  
`dsp:is-pradhana-taught-in-sruti` · skeleton

- **lin:samkhya**: Yes: pradhāna is established by inference and confirmed by scripture; reliable testimony teaches what inference does not reach.
- **lin:vedanta**: No: the scriptural cause 'saw' (reflected) before creating, so the unconscious pradhāna is not scriptural; the passages cited for Sāṃkhya mean something else, and Kapila's smṛti yields where it conflicts with śruti.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: Sāṃkhya enumerates principles analytically to discriminate self from non-self, while the Brahma Sūtra asks what the Upaniṣads teach as the conscious first cause — answers to different questions (rejected by both: Śaṅkara denies pradhāna is scriptural; classical Sāṃkhya denies any conscious director).
  - P4-stage: Advaita accepts the discrimination of self from the non-self as preparatory while rejecting an independent pradhāna.
  - Theistic Sāṃkhya synthesis (Vijñānabhikṣu, recalled): prakṛti as material cause under the Lord taught by śruti.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: each side holds the other's reading of the same scriptures to be wrong.

## RQ-103 — Is Śiva the material cause of the world, or only its efficient cause?  
`dsp:is-siva-the-material-cause` · skeleton

- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: Śiva is only the efficient cause; māyā (and bindu) is the material cause and his power the instrument; if Śiva were the material he would be insentient and changing and would share the world's impurity.
- **lin:tamil-saiva-siddhanta**: The same: the world arises from māyā as its material; the Lord, one with it by pervasion, is not transformed (Civañāṉa Pōtam 1 and its commentaries).
- **lin:kashmir-saivism**: Śiva manifests the world within his own consciousness as a reflection (ābhāsa), by his freedom; he is both its material and efficient cause without loss (as recorded by U19).
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Brahman is both material and efficient cause, the world being its apparent transformation (vivarta) through māyā (as recorded by U13).
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: analytic realism vs absolute non-duality
  - P1-level: at the conventional level māyā is the material; ultimately only Śiva (rejected by the Siddhānta)
- Note: Not yet reconciled; a P2 reading (substance-standpoint realism vs absolute-standpoint non-dualism) is a candidate.

## RQ-105 — Is the ultimate of the nature of speech (śabdādvaita), and is all cognition word-permeated?  
`dsp:is-the-ultimate-speech` · skeleton

- **lin:vyakarana**: Yes: Brahman is the Word-principle appearing as the world; no cognition is free of the word; speech is the consciousness of beings.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: No: an eternal single Word-brahman cannot produce successive, various things; if the world is its transformation it is non-eternal, if its appearance it is unreal; perception is free of words and conceptual construction.
- **lin:digambara**: No: Prabhācandra refutes Word-monism among the doctrines opposed to the Jain view of many real substances and conceptual and non-conceptual cognition.
- **lin:mantrasastra**: Śabda-brahman is not the word nor the meaning, both insentient, but the consciousness of all beings — speech is consciousness, not consciousness speech.
- **lin:pratyabhijna**: Supreme speech is the reflexive awareness of consciousness, not the grammarians' paśyantī.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: the grammarians, the mantra manuals and the Pratyabhijñā may all describe the reflexive, 'speaking' side of consciousness, differing on whether it is the whole of the ultimate.
  - P1-level: word-permeation may hold of conceptual (vyāvahārika) cognition, which the Buddhists also treat as word-bound, while the Buddhists deny it of perception.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: whether reality is Word, and whether cognition is word-permeated.

## RQ-U50-01 — Is there a self (ātman, puruṣa, jīva) — an enduring conscious subject distinct from body and changing cognitions — or only a stream of impersonal factors designated 'person'?  
`dsp:is-there-a-self` · skeleton

- **lin:nyaya**: There is a self: an eternal, all-pervading substance distinct from body, senses, mind and cognitions; cognition, desire, aversion, effort, pleasure and pain are its qualities and the marks by which it is known. To think 'the self does not exist' is itself a form of the false knowledge that binds.
- **lin:vaisesika**: The self is not perceived by ordinary perception but inferred: breathing in and out, the closing and opening of the eyes, life, the movement of the mind, the functioning of the other senses, pleasure, pain, desire, aversion and effort are its marks; the word 'I' applies to it, not to the body; selves are many, since their states differ.
- **lin:mimamsa**: A self distinct from body and cognition exists — the agent who enjoys the fruit of sacrifice in another world; without it the injunctions for heaven would be pointless. Kumārila: the self is known in the notion 'I'; Prabhākara: it shines forth in every cognition as its knower, never as its object.
- **lin:samkhya**: Puruṣa exists: aggregates exist for the sake of another; there must be something opposite to what has the three guṇas; there must be a controller, an enjoyer, and one for whose isolation activity takes place. Puruṣas are many, since birth, death and the instruments are allotted to each separately.
- **lin:patanjala-yoga**: The seer is seeing alone, which appears to conform to the mind's cognitions; the mind is not self-illuminating, because it is seen; if one cognition were grasped by another there would be infinite regress and confusion of memories; a mind that were only momentary cognitions could not be made one-pointed.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: The self is the self-evident witness (sākṣin) of all cognitions, never an object and never deniable — no one thinks 'I am not'. Body, senses, breath, mind and intellect, and the agent-enjoyer, are not-self, superimposed on it. Against the Buddhists: memory and recognition ('I saw that, I see this') show one enduring knower; a momentary store-consciousness cannot hold impressions; the witness is not a momentary cognition.
- **lin:visistadvaita**: The self is a distinct, eternal, atomic knower, established in the awareness 'I', self-luminous; consciousness is its attribute, which belongs to someone and is of something — not the self itself; each self is a mode of Brahman.
- **lin:jainism**: The soul (jīva) exists; its defining mark is cognitive activity (upayoga); it is the doer and enjoyer of its karma, can be liberated, and is eternal as substance but non-eternal as mode (its states and births). One-sided permanence (as of the Naiyāyikas) and one-sided momentariness (as of the Buddhists) are both refuted.
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: There is a soul within the body and distinct from it: it says 'not this' of each thing, speaks of 'my body', knows through the senses, and knows on waking that it slept; souls are many, their experience, bondage and release differing.
- **lin:pratyabhijna**: Against the Buddhist that only momentary cognitions appear and no permanent seer: memory is impossible if the remembering cognition is separate from the experience; the unity of cognitions at different times is the one conscious subject, Maheśvara, endowed with the powers of knowing, remembering and excluding.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: Each of the five aggregates is not-self: were it self it would not lead to affliction and one could say 'let it be thus'; what is impermanent and painful is not fit to be regarded 'this is mine, this I am, this is my self'. 'Being' is a designation, as 'chariot' is for assembled parts. Asked 'is there a self? is there no self?' the Buddha was silent: either answer would side with eternalism or annihilationism.
- **lin:theravada**: The person is not found as a real and ultimate fact; 'Nāgasena' and 'chariot' are designations; as the chariot is not found when each part is examined, there is no being in the ultimate sense, only mentality-materiality.
- **lin:sautrantika**: Apart from the series of aggregates there is no self; 'self' designates the series. Those outside this teaching are not liberated because they hold 'self' to be a separate entity rather than a designation for the series (Vasubandhu, AKBh ch. 9); only aggregates, conditioned by defilement and karma, enter the womb through the intermediate existence, like a flame.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: One who sees a self loves it as 'I', thirsts for its happiness, and that thirst hides faults and drives rebirth: the view of self is the root of saṃsāra. The selves of the Naiyāyikas, Mīmāṃsakas, Sāṃkhyas, Jains, Upaniṣadic thinkers and Vātsīputrīyas are examined and refuted; whatever exists is momentary.
- **lin:madhyamaka**: If the self were the aggregates it would arise and perish; if other than them it would lack their marks. The self is neither the aggregates, nor other than them, nor their possessor — like the chariot sought in seven ways. The Buddhas have taught 'self', have taught 'no-self', and have taught neither self nor no-self; 'I exist' and 'mine exists' are false in the ultimate sense.
- **lin:pudgalavada**: A person (pudgala) exists, designated in dependence on the present aggregates, neither the same as nor different from them, inexpressible — as fire in dependence on fuel; it is the bearer of the burden of the aggregates (the middle position; see dsp:pudgala).
- **lin:tathagatagarbha**: 'Self' in the true sense means the tathāgatagarbha: all beings have buddha-nature; seeing the Tathāgata as impermanent, suffering, non-self and impure is inverted; the dharmakāya has the perfection of self. (Other Mahāyāna texts read this 'self' as a skilful name for emptiness — see dsp:buddha-nature-self-or-emptiness.)
- **lin:carvaka**: (As reported) Only the living body qualified by consciousness; nothing survives it.
- **lin:sramana**: (As reported) Pakudha: the soul is one of seven eternal, unmoving bodies.
- **lin:ajnana**: (As reported) Not declared: 'who knows whether the soul exists?'
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: A self distinct from the body exists: consciousness is absent in the corpse.
- Candidate readings:
  - P1-level: the Buddhist analysis negates a self found among or apart from the aggregates — which Advaita also calls not-self — while Advaita's witness is said never to be an object of such analysis. Rejected by Śāntarakṣita (who refutes the Upaniṣadic self too) and by Advaita (which asserts the self as being-consciousness, not an absence).
  - P2-standpoint (the Jains' own synthesis): permanent from the substance standpoint, momentary from the mode standpoint, so the Nyāya and Buddhist theses each state one aspect. Rejected by both Nyāya and the Buddhists as combining incompatible claims.
  - P4-stage (Kṣemarāja, PH 8): each doctrine of the self is a role of the one consciousness reached at a given level — body, buddhi, prāṇa, void, prakṛti, Īśvara. An inclusivist ranking that the ranked schools do not accept.
  - P5-neyartha: Mahāyāna passages on a 'true self' (tathāgatagarbha) read as provisional by some, definitive by others (see dsp:buddha-nature-self-or-emptiness).
  - P6-upaya: the Buddha's silence to Vacchagotta (SN 44.10) and MMK 18.6 ('self' and 'no-self' both taught) read as teaching fitted to the hearer; Nyāya and Advaita do not accept that their self is merely a teaching device.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: all parties agree that the body, senses and changing cognitions are not the true self, but they disagree whether anything beyond them — an enduring conscious subject — exists.

## RQ-U33-2 — Do good and bad deeds bear fruit (kiriyavāda) or not (akiriyavāda)?  
`dsp:is-there-fruit-of-action` · skeleton

## RQ-U50-10 — Is there an Īśvara — a Lord who makes the world and dispenses the fruits of action? Is he proved by reason, known only from scripture, a special puruṣa for meditation, or not to be accepted at all?  
`dsp:isvara` · skeleton

- **lin:nyaya**: There is an all-knowing, imperishable maker (Īśvara): the world is an effect, like a pot, so it has an intelligent maker; the combination of atoms, the support of the world, the arts and the Veda's authority require him; he brings about the fruit of human effort according to karma (Udayana answers five objections in five clusters).
- **lin:vaisesika**: At the end of Brahmā's life Maheśvara, wishing to give rest to beings wearied by transmigration, dissolves the world, and at his will creation begins again from the atoms according to the unseen merit of souls.
- **lin:patanjala-yoga**: Īśvara is a special puruṣa untouched by afflictions, actions, their fruition and residues, in whom the seed of omniscience is unsurpassed; devotion to him (praṇidhāna) brings samādhi near, since won over by it he favours the yogin — a model and a support, not the world's maker.
- **lin:samkhya**: Īśvara is not established: whether free or bound he could not create; unconscious prakṛti acts for puruṣa's release as unconscious milk flows for the calf; the fruit of action comes from karma without a governor.
- **lin:mimamsa**: There is no creator: no time without action is known; with no beings yet there would be none to pity, and a compassionate creator would make only happy beings; the Veda needs no author; in the rite the sacrifice, not the deity, is principal, and dharma itself yields the fruit.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Brahman as Lord is the cause of the world's origin, maintenance and dissolution, known from scripture alone — the Veda is breathed out by him; he is not partial or cruel, since like rain he gives according to the seeds of beings' karma; the fruit of works comes from him. (Īśvara belongs to Brahman as related to adjuncts.)
- **lin:visistadvaita**: Inference cannot prove a single omniscient maker of the world; Brahman, the Lord with infinite auspicious qualities, qualified by souls and matter, is known only from scripture (śāstrayonitvāt).
- **lin:dvaita**: Viṣṇu alone is the independent reality from whom come creation, maintenance, dissolution, control, knowledge, ignorance, bondage and release.
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: The world undergoes origination, maintenance and dissolution and so has an agent; unconscious māyā and karma cannot organize themselves to give each soul its due, so an omniscient Lord is required.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: There is no permanent means of knowledge or permanent cause: what is permanent cannot produce effects successively; 'being an effect' does not prove a single intelligent maker of mountains and sprouts; the Blessed One is a means of knowledge by having become so, not as an eternal lord (Dharmakīrti, Śāntarakṣita, Ratnakīrti). Belief in Īśvara as cause arises from the error of a permanent self (Vasubandhu).
- **lin:theravada**: There is no god, no Brahmā, who makes this round of rebirths; only bare dhammas occur through the conjunction of conditions; the wheel of becoming is without a maker.
- **lin:jainism**: The maker-god of the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika cannot be: an unembodied maker cannot act, an embodied one would be limited; if he made the world, where was he before and what was his material? — obstinate fancies. Haribhadra: the doctrine that Īśvara is the maker can be accepted in a true sense — the soul that attains the highest perfection is 'Īśvara', and every soul is the 'maker' of its own condition through its karma; a world-creating Lord is refuted.
- **lin:upanisadic**: Some sages say inherent nature is the cause, others time — they are deluded; it is the greatness of God in the world; 'we have found him, the supreme great Lord of lords'.
- **lin:carvaka**: (As reported) The only lord is the king known to the world; variety comes from own-nature.
- **lin:jainism**: Those who say the world was made by gods, Brahmā, Īśvara, pradhāna or the self-born do not know the truth; the world never perishes.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: If Brahmā were lord of the world, why did he make it unhappy and unjust? Creation by God is a tenet that ends in inaction.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint (Haribhadra's own): 'Īśvara is the maker' true in the sense that the perfected soul is Īśvara and each soul makes its own condition by karma — rejected by Nyāya, whose Lord is eternally free and the maker of the world, and it concedes no world-creator.
  - P3-path (Yoga): devotion to Īśvara as one of the means ('or', YS 1.23) for those of devotional temperament, without deciding creation — the non-theistic Sāṃkhya and the Buddhists do not accept an Īśvara even as an object of devotion.
  - P1-level (Advaita): the Lord is real at the empirical level (Brahman with adjuncts), transcended in the ultimate — rejected by the theistic Vedāntas and the Śaivas, for whom the Lord is ultimately real.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, Vedānta and Śaiva Siddhānta affirm a Lord (by inference or by scripture), Yoga admits a special puruṣa for devotion but not as maker, and Sāṃkhya, Mīmāṃsā, the Buddhists and the Jains reject a creator.

## RQ-U34-01 — Does the omniscient (kevalin) take food?  
`dsp:kevalin-eats` · skeleton

- **lin:svetambara**: Yes: while his gross (audārika) body lasts, the omniscient takes food and bears hunger, thirst and the other hardships that arise from feeling-producing karma.
- **lin:yapaniya**: Yes, with systematic arguments (Śākaṭāyana's Kevalibhuktiprakaraṇa).
- **lin:digambara**: No: the omniscient is free of hunger and thirst, has no bodily pleasure or pain, and his body is sustained without morsel-eating.
- **lin:svetambara**: Yes: the embodied omniscient keeps the feeling (vedanīya) karma and a gross body that is sustained by food; scripture narrates Mahāvīra's eating and illness; the Tattvārthasūtra says eleven hardships occur in the Jina.
- **lin:yapaniya**: Yes: the Yāpanīya grammarian Śākaṭāyana's Kevalibhuktiprakaraṇa argues that eating does not contradict omniscience.
- **lin:digambara**: No: eating presupposes desire and the working of delusion, which the omniscient has destroyed; feeling karma without delusion is powerless, like a seed without water; the omniscient's body is sustained without eating; TS 9.11 is figurative.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: the Digambara account speaks of the omniscient as he is in himself (free of desire and suffering, beyond the senses), the Śvetāmbara of the persisting physical body — both agree he has no desire or delusion
  - P1-level: 'eleven in the Jina' holds at the conventional level of the body's karmic state, 'no hunger' at the level of the soul's nature
  - P2-standpoint: the two sects read TS 9.11 from different standpoints (literal fruition of feeling karma vs its powerlessness without delusion).
  - P1-level: Digambaras speak of the omniscient's inner freedom from desire, Śvetāmbaras of the conventional bodily level — but Digambaras deny eating at every level, so this reading is rejected by them.
  - A factual disagreement about the omniscient's body that no principle may dissolve; keep both.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: both sides describe the same omniscient body and state incompatible facts about it.

## RQ-U39-4 — Are the Mahāyāna sūtras the word of the Buddha?  
`dsp:mahayana-buddhavacana` · skeleton

- **lin:mahayana**: Yes: they were taught by the Buddha to bodhisattva assemblies (and some kept by nāgas until beings could receive them); rejecting them is grave karma.
- **lin:theravada**: No: the Buddha's word is what was recited at the councils; the 'Vetulla' (Vaitulya) teachings are not the Buddha's word.

- Candidate readings:
  - P4: sūtras addressed to disciples of higher capacity, not recited in the śrāvaka councils (the Mahāyāna's own account).
  - P5: the Mahāyāna sūtras as the definitive intent of what the earlier sūtras taught provisionally (Mahāyāna reading) — rejected by Theravāda.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: an authenticity question that none of the eight principles decides.

## RQ-146 — Is the root impurity (āṇava mala) a real substance removable only by ritual initiation, or is it ignorance - a contraction of consciousness - removed by knowledge?  
`dsp:mala-substance-or-ignorance` · skeleton

- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: Āṇava mala is a real, beginningless substance (dravya) that covers the soul's powers of knowing and acting; as a substance it cannot be destroyed by knowledge but only by an act - initiation (dīkṣā) empowered by Śiva - after its ripening; the liberated soul becomes equal to Śiva but remains distinct.
- **lin:trika**: Impurity is ignorance, the cause of the sprouting of saṃsāra (MVT 1.23); ignorance is not the absence of knowledge but its incomplete manifestation (TĀ 1.25-26); knowledge is the sole cause of liberation (TĀ 1.22); the āṇava impurity is consciousness losing its freedom (ĪPK 3.2.4); grace does not depend on the ripening of impurity or on a balance of karma but on the Lord's freedom (TĀ 13); initiation works because it is itself a form of knowledge.
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: Mala is a substance (dravya); knowledge cannot remove it any more than knowing a cataract cures it; Śiva removes it through the initiation rite.
- **lin:tamil-saiva-siddhanta**: Āṇava is eternal and is never destroyed; grace makes its power ineffective, like a snake's poison stilled by a mantra; knowledge given by grace (not the soul's own effort) accompanies this.

## RQ-U33-3 — Does anything survive the body's death — is there another world and rebirth, or is consciousness a product of the body that ends with it?

- **lin:sramana**: (As reported) Ajita: the person of four elements is annihilated at death; there is no other world.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: There is another world; the denier loses the wager either way; Kumāra Kassapa answers each of Pāyāsi's arguments with a simile; Śāntarakṣita argues that a first cognition presupposes a prior one, so mind is a beginningless continuum.

## RQ-150 — How many members does a demonstration (inference for others) need?

  - P4-stage / P6-upaya: the number of stated members depends on the hearer's capacity — a principle the Jain side states explicitly and Nyāya approaches by distinguishing inference for oneself (no verbal members) from inference for others.
  - The shared logical core (pervasion plus the reason's presence in the subject) is common to all sides.

## RQ-153 — Is everything that exists momentary?  
`dsp:momentariness` · skeleton

- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: Yes: whatever exists is momentary, since existence is causal efficacy and an enduring thing could produce its effects neither all at once nor successively; recognition is a construction over a series.

- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: momentariness as the standpoint of modes, endurance as that of substance (Jain).
  - P1-level: continuity (santāna) as conventionally valid in Buddhist accounts.
- Note: Not yet reconciled.

- **lin:pasupata**: The Pāśupata path is the best of all paths, the true path, others being bad paths; its scripture comes from Maheśvara through the teachers; it uses the Vedic brahma-mantras and is for brahmins; only from the Pañcārtha comes true ascertainment of reality.
- **lin:puranic**: The Vedic Pāśupata vow of the Atharvaśiras is to be followed; the Pāśupata, Soma, Lākula, Vāma and Bhairava scriptures are non-Vedic and were taught for delusion.
- **lin:visistadvaita**: All four Māheśvara groups are outside the Veda: they contradict the Vedic Brahman as material and efficient cause and devise āśramas outside the Vedic order.
- **lin:epic-teaching**: Pāśupata is one of five systems of knowledge (with Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pañcarātra and the Vedas), taught by Śiva Śrīkaṇṭha; Nārāyaṇa is the foundation in all.
- Candidate readings:
  - P3-path: the Nārāyaṇīya's own stance - several systems for different temperaments with one foundation.
  - P4-stage: the Pāśupata presents itself as a state beyond the āśramas for qualified brahmins (atyāśrama).
  - No reconciliation: the Kūrma Purāṇa and Yāmuna explicitly deny Vedic authority to these scriptures.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the Pāśupatas claim the highest path; Purāṇic and Vaiṣṇava critics place the Pāśupata scriptures outside the Veda.

## RQ-172 — Is Speech at the level of paśyantī (the grammarians' Word-Brahman) the ultimate reality?  
`dsp:pasyanti-brahman` · skeleton

- **lin:vyakarana**: The beginningless and endless Brahman is the principle of the Word (śabdatattva), from which the world unfolds; paśyantī, the undivided 'seeing' speech, is the highest level of speech (Bhartṛhari).
- **lin:pratyabhijna**: What the grammarians call paśyantī is only the knowledge-power at the level of Sadāśiva (Śivadṛṣṭi 2.1); the ultimate is Śiva with will, knowledge and action; speech as reflexive awareness is 'supreme speech' (parā vāk) beyond paśyantī (ĪPK 1.5.13).
- **lin:vyakarana**: Yes: Brahman is the beginningless Word-principle; paśyantī, undivided and with all sequence withdrawn, the inner self-light, is the supreme station of speech; all cognition is word-natured.
- **lin:pratyabhijna**: No: paśyantī is only the power of knowledge at the level of Sadāśiva; the ultimate is Śiva with will, knowledge and action, and supreme speech (parā vāk) is reflexive awareness itself, beyond paśyantī.
- **lin:mantrasastra**: The mantra manuals, accepting the four levels, place parā above paśyantī and define śabda-brahman as the consciousness of all beings rather than as word or meaning.
- Candidate readings:
  - P1-level: both accept paśyantī as a real level of speech; the Śaivas place parā vāk above it, so the question narrows to whether a level above paśyantī is admitted.
  - P2-standpoint: Utpaladeva's parā vāk and Bhartṛhari's Word-principle may name the same reflexive consciousness from different standpoints.
  - P1-level: all accept paśyantī as a real level; the question narrows to whether a level above it (parā) is admitted — later grammarians (Nāgeśa) themselves admit parā.
  - P2-standpoint: Bhartṛhari's word-nature of awareness (pratyavamarśa) and Utpaladeva's reflexive awareness (vimarśa) may name one reflexivity of consciousness from the grammarian's and the Śaiva's standpoints.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: which level of speech is ultimate.

## RQ-U35-pranayama — Does breath-control (prāṇāyāma) serve meditation and liberation?  
`dsp:pranayama-and-liberation` · skeleton

- **lin:patanjala-yoga**: Yes, as a preparatory limb: after posture, regulating the breath thins the covering of the light and makes the mind fit for concentration.
- **lin:hatha-yoga**: Yes: when the breath moves the mind moves; when the breath is still the mind is still — so the breath must be controlled.
- **lin:svetambara**: No, not as a means to liberation: restraining the breath causes pain, pain disturbs the mind, and the laborious filling, holding and expelling distract; stillness comes from indifference, not force (Hemacandra).

  - P2-standpoint: Haribhadra gives prāṇāyāma an inner meaning (expelling outer states, taking in inner ones), a different sense of the same word.
  - A genuine difference over whether forcible breath restraint steadies or disturbs the mind; keep both.

## RQ-U38-01 — Is there a person (pudgala) that is neither the same as nor different from the aggregates?  
`dsp:pudgala` · skeleton

- **lin:pudgalavada**: There is a person, real, designated in dependence on the internal, appropriated, present aggregates — neither identical with them nor different, as fire is designated in dependence on fuel; it is inexpressible, the fifth kind of knowable; it bears the burden of the aggregates and transmigrates.
- **lin:sautrantika**: There is no person apart from the series of aggregates; 'self' and 'person' are designations of that series. A person that is neither real nor a designation is incoherent; the five-knowables list cannot even be stated; the burden-bearer is the earlier aggregates.
- **lin:sarvastivada**: There is no self; only aggregates conditioned by defilements and karma go from life to life through the intermediate existence, like a flame.
- **lin:theravada**: The person is not found as a real and ultimate fact (saccikaṭṭha-paramattha).
- Candidate readings:
  - P1-level: 'person' is valid at the conventional level for both; the dispute concerns only its ultimate status — rejected by the Pudgalavādins, who deny the person is a mere designation.
  - P2-standpoint: the 'inexpressible' as a standpoint neither of identity nor of difference, comparable to other tetralemma answers — rejected by Vasubandhu as a fifth category unknown to the Buddha.
  - P5-neyartha: sūtras speaking of 'a person' (the burden-bearer) are provisional — accepted by the Sarvāstivāda and Theravāda, rejected by the Pudgalavāda.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: both sides accept that the person is spoken of in dependence on the aggregates, but disagree whether it is anything beyond a designation.

## RQ-206 — Can the Veda be a means of knowledge about an already-existing thing (siddha), or only about what is to be done (sādhya / kārya)?

## RQ-U50-04 — Are the individual souls one with Brahman/God, eternally distinct from him and from one another, or both different and non-different?  
`dsp:souls-one-or-distinct` · skeleton

- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: The individual self is Brahman alone: 'that you are', 'I am Brahman', 'this self is Brahman'. The one Lord is the knower of the field in all fields; the plurality and transmigration of souls belong to adjuncts. Of the three early teachers in the Brahmasūtra, Kāśakṛtsna's view — that the supreme Lord himself abides as the individual self — is the one in accord with scripture.
- **lin:dvaita**: Difference is real and eternal — fivefold (pañca-bheda): between the Lord and souls, the Lord and matter, soul and soul, soul and matter, and matter and matter. 'Tat tvam asi' does not teach identity (it is read as 'sa ātmā atat tvam asi', 'you are not that', or of the soul's dependence on and likeness to the Lord); those who, proud with 'I am Brahman', read the Veda as oneness go to darkness. Liberated souls remain distinct and graded.
- **lin:visistadvaita**: Souls are eternal and many and are modes and body of Brahman, inseparable from him (apṛthak-siddhi). In 'tat tvam asi' both words denote Brahman — 'that' as the cause of the world, 'you' as having the soul as its body; scripture's texts of difference, of non-difference and the mediating texts are all true. The self is a part of Brahman as an attribute is part of what it qualifies; the Gītā's 'never was there a time when I was not, nor you' teaches the eternal plurality of selves.
- **lin:bhedabheda**: The soul is a part (aṃśa) of Brahman, different from it by its limiting adjuncts and non-different in essence (Bhāskara: difference adventitious).
- **lin:dvaitadvaita**: Because scripture declares both, the soul is a part of Brahman both different and non-different by nature (svābhāvika), like the snake and its coils; the released soul abides in non-separation from Brahman while remaining a distinct knower.
- **lin:gaudiya-vaisnava**: The souls are the Lord's marginal power, eternally distinct from yet dependent on him — inconceivable difference and non-difference (acintya-bhedābheda).
- **lin:vedanta**: The Brahmasūtra itself records three earlier views on the passages that identify the self with Brahman: Āśmarathya (identity as the fulfilment of the promise that by knowing one all is known), Auḍulomi (identity when the soul departs in liberation), Kāśakṛtsna (the supreme self abides as the individual self). The soul is called a part 'because of the declarations of difference and otherwise'.
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: The Lord is one with souls and yet other than them, and with them — like the soul with the body, the vowel with the consonants; non-duality means inseparability, not identity; the liberated soul becomes equal to Śiva but remains a soul.
- **lin:kashmir-saivism**: Maheśvara alone is the self of all beings, of the form of the universe, full of the undivided awareness 'I'; the liberated becomes (like) Śiva.
- **lin:vaisesika**: Selves are many, not one: though pleasure, pain and cognition arise alike, their distinct states (bondage, release, experience) show plurality.
- **lin:patanjala-yoga**: Though the seen has ceased for the one whose purpose is fulfilled, it has not ceased for others, being common to them: puruṣas are many.
- **lin:epic-teaching**: Asked whether there are many puruṣas or one, the Mokṣadharma reports that the Sāṃkhya-Yoga thinkers hold many, and teaches that all are grounded in the one Puruṣa.
- **lin:jainism**: Souls are infinitely many, each an independent substance; absolute non-duality is refuted.
- Candidate readings:
  - P1-level (Advaita's own): difference is real for the empirical self, identity is the ultimate truth — rejected by Madhva, for whom difference is real in every state, and by Rāmānuja, who denies identity of essence.
  - P2-standpoint: souls one with the Lord as consciousness (substance standpoint), many as individual centres (mode standpoint) — the bhedābheda schools' own form; rejected by Dvaita as a denial of real difference and by Advaita as a denial of non-duality.
  - P3-path (devotional vs knowledge): the devotee's 'I am yours' (dāsoham) and the knower's 'I am that' (so'ham) as two paths — Śāṇḍilya Bhakti Sūtra 29-31 records Kāśyapa (difference), Bādarāyaṇa (identity) and Śāṇḍilya (both); rejected by Dvaita as making difference merely devotional.
  - P4-stage: dāsoham ripening into so'ham (see dsp:dasoham-soham) — rejected by Dvaita and Viśiṣṭādvaita, for whom the servant relation is eternal.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: identity (Advaita), eternal difference (Dvaita) and qualified or natural difference-in-non-difference (Viśiṣṭādvaita, Bhedābheda, Śaiva Siddhānta) are each defended as the purport of the same texts.

## RQ-214 — Is there a sphoṭa — a unitary word-entity distinct from the letters — that conveys meaning?  
`dsp:sphota` · skeleton

- **lin:vyakarana**: Yes. The letters, perishing one after another, can convey meaning neither singly nor together; the word is a partless, sequence-less sphoṭa manifested by the sounds, grasped when the last sound, helped by the impressions of the earlier ones, makes it clear; finally only the sentence-sphoṭa is real.
- **lin:mimamsa**: No. 'The letters alone are the word' (Upavarṣa): the last letter, together with the impressions left by the earlier ones, conveys the meaning; a sphoṭa is never perceived and is a needless assumption.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: Śaṅkara follows Upavarṣa against the sphoṭa, while accepting that the world is preceded by the Vedic word (words as eternal universals).
- **lin:nyaya**: No separate sphoṭa: the letters are apprehended in sequence and their meaning is understood from the final letter with the recollection of the earlier ones; sound is a non-eternal quality of ether.
- **lin:mimamsa**: No: the word 'gauḥ' is the letters g, au, ḥ (Upavarṣa); meaning arises from the letters (through the impression of the earlier ones), as fire is known from smoke; a sphoṭa is never perceived and is superfluous.
- **lin:vyakarana**: Yes: the letters, perishing in sequence, cannot jointly convey meaning; the indivisible sphoṭa, manifested by the sounds, conveys it (Bhartṛhari); Maṇḍana Miśra's Sphoṭasiddhi defends this against Kumārila.
- Candidate readings:

  - P1-level: Bhartṛhari's sphoṭa belongs to the level of the undivided Word-principle, the letters to the level of articulated speech (vaikharī).
  - P2-standpoint: the letter-sequence (as heard) and the unitary word (as understood) may be two standpoints on one process — the Mīmāṃsakas reject a separate entity, the grammarians a merely serial conveyance.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: whether a unitary meaning-bearer exists over and above the letters.

## RQ-U50-11 — What is the status of the Veda: authorless and eternal (apauruṣeya), breathed out or composed by the omniscient Lord, or without authority?  
`dsp:status-of-veda` · skeleton

- **lin:mimamsa**: The Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya) and eternal: the relation of word and meaning is original, not made by any person; names in the Veda are of expounders, not authors, or mere similarity of sound; since faults in testimony arise from dependence on a speaker, a speakerless Veda is free of them and its cognitions are never overturned; every study of the Veda is preceded by the teacher's study; no omniscient author can be established.
- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: The Veda is eternal — no independent author of it is remembered, and the world with its fixed forms arises from the Vedic word; yet Brahman is its source, from which it is breathed out effortlessly, and it is the only means of knowing Brahman. For the knower, in the highest state, 'the Vedas are no Vedas'; the Veda is the lower knowledge, the knowledge of the imperishable the higher.
- **lin:dvaita**: The Veda is authorless; no author of it is known, and to assume one would be needless; the Ṛg and other Vedas, the Bhārata and the Pañcarātra are the scriptures that teach Viṣṇu.
- **lin:nyaya**: The Veda is valid because it is the word of a reliable, omniscient speaker (the Lord) — like mantras and Āyurveda, whose validity is seen; the charges of falsehood, self-inconsistency and repetition fail. Nyāya is foremost among sciences because it protects the Veda's authority.
- **lin:vaisesika**: The authority of the sacred tradition follows from its being the statement of 'that' (the Lord / the seer); the composition of sentences in the Veda is preceded by understanding.
- **lin:samkhya**: The Vedas are not eternal, since scripture speaks of their being produced; nor are they of personal authorship, since there is no author; their validity is intrinsic.
- **lin:epic-teaching**: The Gītā: the Vedas have the three guṇas as their domain — be free of the three; for the knowing brāhmaṇa there is as much use in all the Vedas as in a well when there is flood all around; yet the one who casts aside scripture's injunctions attains neither perfection nor happiness; 'I alone am to be known by all the Vedas'.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: An authorless Veda cannot be shown true in meaning: truth of speech depends on the qualities of a speaker, and without a speaker there is no guarantee; the Mīmāṃsā denial of any being who sees supersensible things is refuted; the Veda's claims about the supersensible have no means of knowledge behind them.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: The brāhmaṇas versed in the three Vedas teach a path to union with Brahmā though neither they nor their teachers to the seventh generation have seen Brahmā — like a line of blind men.
- **lin:jainism**: The Vedas, the epics and other non-Jain scriptures are 'false scripture' (mithyāśruta) for one of wrong view, though they can serve right knowledge for one of right view; the Jain twelve-limbed canon 'never was not, never is not, never will not be'. Not by the syllable Oṃ is one a brāhmaṇa, but by conduct.
- **lin:mantramarga**: The Veda and the Āgama are both true, both the Lord's scripture — one general, the other special (Tirumantiram); Śrīkaṇṭha sees no difference between the Veda and the Śivāgama; the Kaula texts make the Vedic auxiliaries the limbs of the Kula (see dsp:agama-veda-authority).
- **lin:sant**: Do not say the Vedas and the Books are false; false is the one who does not reflect (Kabīr); I speak of what I have seen, you of what is written on paper; the Bāuls follow the present (bartamān), not the inferred; Basava will put the Veda to the touchstone; Tukārām: we alone know the Veda's meaning, others carry its burden.
- **lin:carvaka**: (As reported) Rejected: the Veda is tainted by falsehood, inconsistency and repetition; its makers were buffoons, knaves and demons; its rites are a livelihood of priests; the Bārhaspatyas call it a mere cover.
- **lin:sramana**: (As reported) Ajita: there is no gift, no sacrifice, no offering.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: Sacrifice of animals and claims of birth-purity are refuted; the priests are cheats (Bhūridatta).
- **lin:nyaya**: The charge of falsehood, inconsistency (vyāghāta) and repetition fails: a failed rite is due to defects in the act, agent or means.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint (Vedānta's middle): from the standpoint of its source the Veda issues from the omniscient; from the standpoint of its form and sequence it is never freely composed — rejected by Mīmāṃsā (no Lord) and by Nyāya (the Lord composes it anew).
  - P1-level / P4-stage (the Upaniṣads' and Gītā's own relativization): the Veda is the lower knowledge, of use until the flood of knowledge — for the knower 'the Vedas are no Vedas'; Mīmāṃsā does not accept a stage beyond the injunctions.
  - P7-arthavada: the Sants' and Tantras' disparagements of Veda-reciters read as censure of misuse, not of the Veda (Kabīr: 'do not say the Vedas are false') — the śramaṇa critics reject the Veda itself, not only its misuse.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the Vedic schools agree the Veda is valid but disagree whether it has an author (Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta vs Nyāya), and the śramaṇa, Cārvāka and some bhakti and Sant voices deny or relativize its authority.

## RQ-222 — Can Mahāmudrā be introduced and realized apart from tantric empowerment — Gampopa's 'sūtra Mahāmudrā', the 'white self-sufficient remedy' — or is Mahāmudrā only the wisdom arising from empowerment and the two stages?  
`dsp:sutra-mahamudra` · skeleton

- **lin:kagyu**: Mahāmudrā is the nature of one's own mind; a realized guru can point it out through blessing to a ripe disciple, also on the basis of the sūtra teaching of buddha-nature, and recognizing it suffices ('the white self-sufficient remedy'); the tantric path of method is a second, complementary path, not the only door.
- **lin:sakya**: Mahāmudrā, in the tantras, is the wisdom born from the empowerments and the completion stage; a 'Mahāmudrā' taught without them is not the Buddha's Mahāmudrā; the 'white self-sufficient remedy' is the teaching of the Chinese Hashang, refuted at Samye; the path needs the gradual accumulation of merit and wisdom.
- **lin:gelug**: There is both a sūtra Mahāmudrā — the emptiness of mind realized by a mind that has established emptiness by reasoning — and a tantric Mahāmudrā — the clear light of great bliss; the Ganden (Gelug) Mahāmudrā tradition teaches both (First Panchen Lama).
- **lin:sakya**: Sakya Paṇḍita: the Mahāmudrā taught by the Buddha is only the wisdom born of empowerment and the two stages; the present-day 'white self-sufficient remedy' taught without empowerment is the Chinese (Hwashang) Great Perfection under another name, and a fool's meditation on it leads to animal or formless rebirth or the śrāvaka cessation.
- **lin:kagyu**: The Kagyu teach a Mahāmudrā of pointing out the nature of mind that can be introduced on the basis of sūtra (the Ratnagotravibhāga and Maitrīpa's teaching of non-attention), as Gampopa did; later Kagyu scholars answered Sakya Paṇḍita that his target was not their teaching.
- **lin:ganden-oral-lineage**: The First Panchen Lama: Mahāmudrā has a sūtra approach (emptiness of mind as Nāgārjuna taught) and a mantra approach (clear light of great bliss); the various Mahāmudrā instructions converge in one intent.
- Candidate readings:
  - P4-stage: 'sūtra Mahāmudrā' is for persons of the simultaneous type with prior training (Ājñāsamyakpramāṇa 271a.3); Sakya objection — the Hashang made the same claim.
  - P1-level / terminological: the word names two things — the emptiness-luminosity of mind recognized through blessing, and the tantric wisdom of the fourth empowerment; if so the dispute is partly about the name; Sakya objection — the name is proper only to the tantric referent; Kagyu objection — the referent is one.
  - P6-upaya: blessing functions as an empowerment for the ripe disciple; Sakya objection — formal empowerment is required.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the sides disagree whether the guru's blessing without formal empowerment can be the cause of Mahāmudrā realization.

## RQ-225 — Is the validity of a cognition intrinsic (svataḥ) or extrinsic (parataḥ)?  
`dsp:svatah-paratah-pramanya` · skeleton

- **lin:nyaya**: Validity is extrinsic: it is produced by an excellence in the causes of a cognition and is known not with the cognition itself but by inference — from the success of the activity it prompts or other marks; invalidity likewise comes from defects and is known extrinsically.
- **lin:mimamsa**: Every cognition is valid by itself: validity arises from the very conditions that produce the cognition and is apprehended with it; invalidity is extrinsic, known from a defect in the causes or a later sublating cognition. A power not present in a thing by itself cannot be produced by another.
- **lin:samkhya**: Both validity and invalidity are intrinsic — as reported in later doxography.
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: Invalidity is intrinsic and validity extrinsic — as reported in the same doxographic scheme (Buddhist sources themselves are more differentiated).
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: Validity is known through practical activity (vyavahāra) — whether the cognition leads to the object's efficacy (Dharmakīrti, PV Pramāṇasiddhi 5, conventional).
- **lin:pramana-buddhist**: There is no single rule: some cognitions (self-awareness, inference, habituated perception, cognitions of efficacy) are valid by themselves, others only extrinsically (Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla, as recalled).
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: the Mīmāṃsaka thesis may describe the default acceptance of a cognition when no doubt has arisen, the Naiyāyika thesis the justification of validity once doubt arises.
  - P2-standpoint: both distinguish the arising (utpatti) from the ascertainment (jñapti) of validity; agreement may be possible on the first and not the second.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the schools disagree about how validity is established.

## RQ-U14-01 — What divides the northern (Vaṭakalai) and southern (Teṅkalai) schools of Śrīvaiṣṇavism — the 'eighteen differences' (aṣṭādaśa-bheda)?  
`dsp:vadakalai-tenkalai` · skeleton

- **lin:vadakalai**: Surrender is a means the soul performs once, with its limbs, and the occasion of the Lord's grace ('the young monkey clings'); Śrī is all-pervading, of the Lord's rank, and with him the means and goal; the Lord overlooks faults rather than relishing them; scriptural duties bind the surrendered as the Lord's command, and deliberate offences need expiation; kaivalya is not permanent.
- **lin:tenkalai**: The Lord alone is the means; surrender is not a means but acceptance fitting the soul's dependence ('the kitten is carried'); grace is causeless; Śrī is the mediator, not the means; the Lord relishes his devotees' faults; duties are kept as service and example, not as means or under threat; the teacher's regard saves; kaivalya is permanent.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: the Teṅkalai speak from the divine standpoint (the Lord alone saves), the Vaṭakalai from the seeker's (surrender is what the seeker does)
  - P4-stage: the Teṅkalai stress what fits the utterly helpless; the Vaṭakalai what scripture enjoins on the qualified
  - shared ground: both hold the Lord as siddhopāya, surrender once, no last remembrance needed, release at the end of the body, eternal service
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the schools disagree on whether the soul's surrender is a means and on Śrī's rank; see the sub-disputes, some partially reconciled.

## RQ-240 — Is inference a valid means of knowledge?  
`dsp:validity-of-inference` · skeleton

- **lin:carvaka**: No (as reported by opponents): only perception is valid; pervasion cannot be established — perception cannot survey all cases, inference would presuppose inference, and testimony is itself inferential; Jayarāśi's scepticism extends even to perception.

`dsp:validity-of-pancaratra` · skeleton

- **lin:mimamsa**: The Pāñcarātra is invalid: not rooted in the Veda, practised by Sātvatas/Bhāgavatas who are a low temple-serving class, classed by smṛti with heretical systems, and refuted by Bādarāyaṇa.

## RQ-242 — Is varṇa (and eligibility for austerity and teaching) fixed by birth, or made by conduct and qualities?  
`dsp:varna-by-birth-or-conduct-epic` · skeleton

- **lin:epic-teaching**: Birth and nature: the varṇas' duties arise from their nature and one should not abandon the work born with one (BhG 18.41–48; 3.35); service is the śūdra's dharma (MBh 12.60.27–28; 5.40.26); Vidura, born of a śūdra womb, will not teach beyond this (5.41.5); in the Rāmāyaṇa the śūdra Śambūka's austerity is held to cause a child's death and Rāma kills him (7.74–76).
- **lin:epic-teaching**: Conduct and qualities: one in whom truth, generosity, forbearance, good conduct, non-cruelty, restraint and compassion are seen is a brāhmaṇa (3.177.16); the whole world is of Brahman and came to varṇa by actions (12.181.10); the varṇas were made by guṇa and action (BhG 4.13); knowledge may be taken even from a śūdra (12.306.85); a meat-seller teaches a brāhmaṇa (3.198–206) and a merchant an ascetic (12.253–256); the wise see the same in a brāhmaṇa and a dog-eater (BhG 5.18); women, vaiśyas and śūdras too reach the highest goal (9.32).

  - bhakti reading: devotion and refuge open liberation to all regardless of birth (BhG 9.32; 18.66), while social duties remain
  - yuga reading (Rāmāyaṇa 7.74): eligibility for austerity differs by age of the world
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the epics record both a birth-based order of duties and eligibility and repeated teachings that conduct and qualities make varṇa and that liberation is open to all.

`dsp:vedic-ritual-killing-and-ahimsa` · sourced

  - P4-stage reading of MDh 5.56: ritual meat is permitted at the level of pravṛtti, abstention is the higher nivṛtti
  - yuga-dharma: later authorities forbid animal (cow) sacrifice in the Kali age
  - Brāhmaṇa substitution: the sacrificial essence passed from animals into rice and barley (ŚB 1.2.3.6–9)

In the Manīṣāpañcaka, whoever has realized the one consciousness is the teacher, be he caṇḍāla or brāhmaṇa.
- **lin:upanisadic**: Maitreyī was a discusser of Brahman; Satyakāma, who did not know his father, was accepted as a brāhmaṇa for his truthfulness; Jānaśruti, addressed as 'śūdra', was taught by Raikva.
- **lin:digambara**: Women cannot attain liberation in a female body: they cannot give up clothing and so cannot be without possessions; they lack the strongest bodily constitution needed for the highest pure meditation; as they cannot fall to the lowest hell they cannot rise to the highest state; the scripture's 'female siddhas' means psychological, not physical, sex (Prabhācandra); subtle living beings arise in their bodies (Aṣṭapāhuḍa). The monk's sign is 'like the newborn' — naked.
- **lin:svetambara**: Women can attain liberation: the nineteenth Tīrthaṅkara, Mallī, was a woman; the liberated include those liberated in a female form (Prajñāpanā's fifteen kinds); nuns keep their robes by monastic law.
- **lin:yapaniya**: Śākaṭāyana: there is liberation for women as for men, since its cause — the three jewels — is present in them; a woman is not a non-soul, not incapable of liberation, not opposed to right faith, not non-human.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: Women who go forth are capable of the fruits up to arahantship (the Buddha to Ānanda), though it is impossible that a woman be a fully awakened Buddha; Mahāpajāpatī's ordination came with eight principles of respect. Somā: what does womanhood matter when the mind is concentrated? Class is not by birth: among humans there is no difference of species; one is a brāhmaṇa by deeds.
- **lin:mahayana**: The nāga king's eight-year-old daughter attains buddhahood (the Lotus Sūtra, in which she turns into a man); the goddess in Vimalakīrti's house shows Śāriputra that 'woman' has no fixed mark; Amitābha's thirty-fifth vow; in Zen Dōgen urges bowing to anyone who has attained the Way, man or woman.
- **lin:epic-teaching**: The Gītā: those who take refuge in me, even of sinful birth — women, vaiśyas and śūdras — reach the highest goal; the learned see the same in a brāhmaṇa, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a dog-eater.
- **lin:puranic**: Devotion knows no distinction of caste, learning, beauty, family or wealth; those of every birth down to the lowest are qualified for it; a dog-cooker who has offered all to the Lord is better than a learned brāhmaṇa without devotion; Rāma tells Śabarī that neither sex, caste, name nor stage of life is the cause of worship.
- **lin:alvar**: The Lord's devotees, even the lowest of the low below the four castes, are to be honoured as he is; the pāṇar Tiruppāṇ Āḻvār was carried into Śrīraṅgam (the tradition's account).
- **lin:virasaiva**: Wherever devotion to Śiva is strong, low or high, that one is a devotee; among those joined in the one contemplation of Śiva there is no brāhmaṇa or outcaste. Dāsimayya: they call it woman if they see breasts and long hair, man if beard and whiskers — but the self in between is neither.
- **lin:sant**: No one is low by birth; the mud of low deeds makes one low (Ravidās); do not ask a sādhu's caste — ask his knowledge (Kabīr). (The same Kabīr corpus also contains sayings warning against women as a snare — recorded, not harmonised.)
- **lin:tamil-siddha**: What makes a woman a Paṟaicci or a brāhmaṇī — is there any difference in flesh, skin or bone? (Civavākkiyar); learning, caste distinctions and ritual observances are to be burnt away (Pāmpāṭṭi); mankind is one family and God is one (Tirumantiram).
- **lin:kaula**: When the Bhairavī circle has begun, all castes are twice-born; when it is over they are separate again; no caste distinction is to be made in the consecrated food of the supreme Self.
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: The Kiraṇa, asked whether initiation should follow the ranking of the classes: for women and others incapable of the observances, the guru purifies the observances away and their liberation is through the yoga of devotion in the Śaiva scripture; for the capable the observances remain and knowledge is revealed to them.
- **lin:hatha-yoga**: Young, old or very old, sick or weak — whoever is diligent attains success in yoga through practice; women and śūdras too may practise breath-control, with a non-Vedic mantra (Yoga-Yājñavalkya).
- Candidate readings:
  - P4-stage (adhikāra): eligibility for particular disciplines (Vedic study, the naked vow) differs by birth or body while liberation itself does not — the orthodox and Digambara form; rejected by the bhakti, Sant, Vīraśaiva and Siddha critics as a restatement of birth-rank, and by the Digambaras as to liberation itself.
  - P2-standpoint: from the standpoint of the self (neither male nor female — Dāsimayya; the Advaita witness) no distinction; from the standpoint of the body and social order, distinctions — each side accepts only its own half.
  - P3-path: devotion open to all (NBS 72; BhG 9.32) as a distinct path from Vedic knowledge — accepted by many Vedāntins (Rāmānuja on 9.32) while retaining Vedic restrictions; rejected by the Sants as insufficient.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: the orthodox Vedic order restricts eligibility for Vedic study and rite by birth and sex, the Digambaras deny liberation in a female body, while the Śvetāmbaras and Yāpanīyas, the Buddhists, the bhakti, Vīraśaiva, Sant and Siddha voices and the Kaula tantras open liberation (and in varying degrees initiation) to all.

## RQ-U50-03 — Is the world real, or an appearance (mithyā) that is neither real nor unreal, superimposed on the one reality — and is māyā an indeterminable ignorance or the Lord's real power?  
`dsp:world-real-or-appearance` · skeleton

- **lin:advaita-vedanta**: The world is mithyā: not real, since it is sublated by the knowledge of Brahman; not unreal, since it appears; indeterminable as either — like the silver seen in shell. Duality is mere māyā; non-duality is the ultimate truth. Three orders are distinguished: the ultimate (Brahman), the empirical (the world, real for all practical purposes — Śaṅkara defends external objects against the Buddhist idealist), and the illusory (dream, rope-snake). Falsity is defended under five definitions against the Dvaita critique.
- **lin:visistadvaita**: The world of souls and matter is real, the body of Brahman. The Advaitin's avidyā is untenable in seven ways — no locus, no power to conceal self-luminous Brahman, no coherent nature, no sense to 'indescribable', no means of knowledge, no remover, no removal; no means of knowledge establishes an undifferentiated reality; consciousness is always someone's and of something.
- **lin:dvaita**: The world is real: the witness (sākṣin) establishes it as unsublated; what is sublated produces no effect; even dream-objects are real products of impressions. None of the five Advaita definitions of falsity is coherent, and the inferences from 'perceivable', 'insentient' and 'limited' fail. Reality is twofold: the independent (Viṣṇu) and the dependent (souls and matter) — dependent, but real.
- **lin:bhedabheda**: The effect is real and both different and non-different from its cause; those who expound māyā lead people astray (Bhāskara).
- **lin:gaudiya-vaisnava**: The world is a real transformation of the Lord's external power; the souls are his marginal power, distinct yet dependent. To call the world false and the Lord's lordship imagined is the māyāvāda that the Gītā (16.8) describes and the Padma Purāṇa calls 'Buddhism in disguise'.
- **lin:kashmir-saivism**: The universe is the real self-manifestation of free consciousness on its own screen; māyā is the Lord's power of self-concealment (Māyā-śakti), his supreme freedom — not an indeterminable ignorance. Those who say 'in the beginning this was being' stand at the level of the Īśvara-tattva, and some knowers of the Upaniṣads take prāṇa as the self: partial roles of the one consciousness. (The Vijñāna Bhairava nevertheless teaches contemplating the universe as a magic show as one means to peace.)
- **lin:sakta**: Māyā is the Goddess's own power; she is the manifest and the unmanifest; her reality is beyond both duality and non-duality (see dsp:maya-sakti-or-avidya).
- **lin:saiva-siddhanta**: The world is real, produced from real māyā by Śiva as efficient cause; 'advaita' in the Śivajñānabodha means non-separateness, not numerical identity; the Advaita and other views are set out and refuted.
- **lin:samkhya**: The world is real, being produced by faultless causes and never sublated; bondage cannot be due to an unreal avidyā — and a real avidyā would abandon non-dualism.
- **lin:jainism**: Absolute non-duality is refuted: if all were one there would be no distinction of act and fruit, merit and demerit, this world and the next, knowledge and ignorance, bondage and release.
- **lin:nyaya**: The idealist and illusionist thesis lacks a reason: the notion of dream-objects depends on prior waking experience, as memory does; false cognition presupposes a real object mistaken.
- **lin:madhyamaka**: All dharmas, the fruits of the path and buddhahood itself are like an illusion, like a dream — appearing conventionally, empty of own-nature; form is emptiness and emptiness form (the Prajñāpāramitā background shared by the Mahāyāna schools).
- Candidate readings:
  - P1-level (Advaita's own): the world is real at the empirical level and false only from the standpoint of Brahman — rejected by Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita, for whom whatever is, is real, and the empirical/ultimate distinction is not scriptural.
  - P2-standpoint: from the side of the one consciousness there is no second, from the side of the embodied soul the world is real — rejected by Madhva and Rāmānuja, who hold the world real from every standpoint, and by Advaita if it implies that the world's reality is ultimate.
  - P4-stage: Kṣemarāja ranks the Vedāntins' 'being' at the Īśvara-tattva, and the Vijñāna Bhairava prescribes seeing the world as a magic show as a practice (VBT 102, 133) — a practice-level use of the 'appearance' view that does not settle its truth.
  - vivarta vs ābhāsa (Advaita vs Kashmir Śaivism): both deny that the one consciousness changes; they differ whether its appearing is real self-display or indeterminable — kept apart by both.
- Note: Not yet reconciled: Advaita's own levels of reality make the world real empirically and false ultimately, but Viśiṣṭādvaita, Dvaita, Śaiva Siddhānta and Kashmir Śaivism reject the very distinction as applied to the world, each for its own reasons.

## RQ-258 — Did Pātañjala Yoga take its shared meditative and ethical vocabulary (the four attitudes, the five faculties, the afflictions, samāpatti, dharmamegha) from Buddhism, or do Yoga, Buddhism and Jainism draw on a common śramaṇa milieu?  
`dsp:yoga-buddhism-shared-milieu` · skeleton

- **lin:patanjala-yoga**: Yoga is an ancient teaching (the tradition names Hiraṇyagarbha as its first teacher and Patañjali as its compiler); the Yoga text marks its difference from the Buddhists, rejecting the view that objects are constructions of a single mind; no dependence on Buddhism is acknowledged.
- **lin:early-buddhism**: The suttas themselves acknowledge that other wanderers taught the abandoning of the hindrances and the four immeasurables, and that the bodhisatta learned the formless attainments from other teachers; the Buddha's distinctiveness lies in knowing where these culminate and in the liberating insight beyond them.
- **lin:jainism**: The five great vows and the four reflections belong to the Jina's teaching; Haribhadra treats the yoga taught by others as one path of qualities under different names, correlating his eight views with the limbs beginning with yama.
- Candidate readings:
  - Common milieu: the object-class form of the four attitudes (YS 1.33 = TS 7.11, unlike the Pali formula) and the shared five vows suggest a common śramaṇa stock rather than one-way borrowing.
  - Specific borrowing: technical terms of Buddhist Abhidharma (anuśaya, the burnt seed, dharmamegha as a ground-name) may have been taken over by the Yoga authors (scholarly hypothesis).
  - P2-standpoint (doctrinal content only): the same practices described from different standpoints — boundless pervasion (Buddhist) versus attitudes suited to classes of beings (Yoga, Jain).
- Note: Not yet reconciled: a historical question on which the traditions' own accounts and the scholarly hypotheses differ; no principle settles history.

## RQ-U32-yuga-quarters — Are the four quarters of the yuga equal, or in the proportion 4:3:2:1?  
`dsp:yuga-quarters` · skeleton

- **lin:jyotisa**: Āryabhaṭa: the four quarters of the yuga are equal; the kalpa has 14 Manus of 72 yugas; the yuga has an ascending and a descending half.
- **lin:jyotisa**: Brahmagupta and the Sūrya Siddhānta: Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara and Kali are four, three, two and one tenths of the yuga, following the feet of dharma; 'not one of Āryabhaṭa's quarters agrees with the smṛti'.
- Candidate readings:
  - P2-standpoint: Āryabhaṭa's equal quarters serve computation (gaṇita) while the 4:3:2:1 scheme expresses the decline of dharma (smṛti) — but Brahmagupta explicitly rejects this separation.
  - Brahmagupta's reading: Āryabhaṭa simply erred against scripture.
  - Recent: Sri Yukteswar's 24,000-year cycle (The Holy Science, 1894) departs from both (recent: true).
- Note: Not yet reconciled.
